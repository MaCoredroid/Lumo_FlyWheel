Volume VIII / illustrated systems paper

Every Lever We Pulled

A Speed Campaign in Full — Sixteen Attempts to Make a Branched Speculative Tree Faster, What Each One Actually Did, and the Measurement That Decided It

Vol. VII left the branched token-tree accepting more tokens than native linear decode and losing on throughput anyway. This volume is the campaign that followed: nine hundred and eighty-four commits of speed work, reported as an inventory rather than a narrative. Every attempt gets the same four questions — what observation motivated it, what mechanism it used, what it measurably did, and what evidence decided it. Eight levers paid and are baked; eight did not, including one that consumed thirty-five boots and delivered a real engineering milestone with no speed at all. Throughput went from 32.9 to 43.6 tokens per second, and the attempts collectively revealed something none of them was looking for: the deficit had changed shape, from a per-token tax into a fixed per-step one.

TL;DR: an inventory of a speed campaign. What paid: a truncated drafter head (drafter 94.9 → 56.3 ms/step at unchanged acceptance), whole-spine drafter graph capture (~−40 ms), a batched all-layer committer (span 47 → 36 ms), in-kernel replay staging (32.85 → 38.01 tok/s), a sync-free KV remap (committer to 12.33 ms/event), a subtree-parallel scan exploiting the independence of disjoint branches (−22.3 ms/event against a +32.7 ms fixed cost, crossing over well below where we run), and fused-then-batched conv write-back (−20 ms/step). What didn't: capturing the whole speculative region into one CUDA graph — thirty-five boots, a genuine milestone, and a speed effect below the detection floor because the prerequisite for capture had already removed the cost it targeted; overlap and async hiding, dead at deployment because the GPU is already 97% busy; single-streaming the subtree scan, refuted by a source read before any build; cutting the deep tail, an anti-lever because the tail carries most of the acceptance; and four more. What it added up to: the tree's per-event cost, once ~140 ms/event against native's 49, is now statistically indistinguishable from native's, and the whole remaining deficit is fixed — which is why the accept-beyond-5 work became a direct win condition.

Abstract

This volume is an inventory of a speculative-decoding speed campaign. Over six days and nine hundred and eighty-four commits we tried sixteen distinct ways to make a branched token-tree decoder faster on a Gated-DeltaNet hybrid, and this page reports all sixteen at comparable depth — the observation that motivated each, the mechanism it used, the effect it measurably had, and the evidence that decided it. Eight are now baked as defaults. Eight are not, and their post-mortems are the more transferable half of the document.

The attempts that paid share a pattern: they removed host and launch structure rather than arithmetic. Capturing the whole spine draft into a CUDA graph, drafting from a truncated output head so the drafter reads half the weights while the verifier keeps the full head, committing all forty-eight layers in one batched pass instead of forty-eight calls, staging replay inputs inside the kernel, removing host synchronizations from the KV remap, and batching the convolution write-back across concurrent requests. The one attempt with genuinely new structure is a subtree-parallel scan: a recurrent scan is sequential along a path, but a tree is only sequential along each path, so its disjoint branches can be scanned concurrently — worth −22.3 ms per event against a +32.7 ms fixed topology cost, crossing over far below our operating point.

The attempts that failed failed in instructive ways. The largest — capturing the entire speculative region into a single CUDA graph — consumed thirty-five boots, produced a real engineering milestone, and moved throughput by less than the detection floor, because making the capture legal had already required hoisting out the host cost the capture was supposed to eliminate. An entire class of overlap and async-hiding levers turned out to be dead at the deployment point, where the GPU is already 97% busy and most steps mix prefill with decode. One fix was refuted by reading the source before anything was built. One proposed saving would have cut the deep suffix tail, which turned out to be carrying most of the acceptance it was competing with.

Collectively the campaign also produced a result none of its attempts was aiming at. Fitting step time against speculative events per step — step = F + m × eps — across seven tree arms puts the tree's marginal per-event cost within a few milliseconds of native's, with an interval that excludes the ~140 ms/event row tax measured earlier in the series. The deficit has moved from the slope into the intercept: roughly 85–90 ms per step that does not scale with acceptance. That reframing is worth more than any single lever, because a fixed cost is diluted by a better speculator where a marginal one is aggravated by it — which turns the accept-beyond-5 work from a nice-to-have into a direct win condition. Two silent bugs and three measurement reversals, all documented here, are the reason it took this long to be able to say so.

How every attempt in this paper is reported: observation — the measurement that suggested there was time to recover; mechanism — what the change actually does to the work; effect — the measured delta, in the units it was measured in; evidence — the gate that decided it, and its basis. Nothing here ships on a speed number alone: every state-touching lever must first be byte-identical to the path it replaces on an offline gate, then land inside the campaign's live pass/fail band garble-free, and only then is its speed signal admissible. Several attempts below produced a speed number and were still rejected, and one produced no speed number and was kept.

Contribution

A complete, comparably-reported speed campaign: sixteen attempts on a branched speculative decoder, with mechanism, measured effect, and deciding evidence for each — failures at the same length as successes. Plus the structural finding they collectively produced, that the decoder's deficit lives in a fixed per-step term rather than a per-event one, which changes what is worth optimizing next.

This page is the sequel to Vol. VII and is self-contained. It is written as a ledger on purpose. Most published performance work reports the levers that worked, which makes the field's failure knowledge private; the eight misses here — including a thirty-five-boot capture effort and an entire lever class that only exists at batch one — are the part we would most want to have read before starting.

How We Judged an Attempt

The order matters: correctness gates run before speed gates, and a speed number from an arm that failed correctness is not a number at all. Two arms in this campaign produced attractive speed figures that were struck from the record for exactly that reason.

Every verdict in the two ledgers below rests on the same pipeline, and it is worth stating before the results because several attempts were decided by the pipeline rather than by their own merits.

Why acceptance is quoted next to every speed number

Throughput here is committed tokens over wall time, and both terms move with how much the speculator accepts. A run with higher acceptance takes longer per step and produces more tokens per step, so comparing bare tokens-per-second across two arms that accepted differently compares their workloads, not their code. This campaign learned that the expensive way: a convincing ranking of CUDA-graph modes turned out to be one regression line seen at four different acceptance rates, and it had been justifying work that moved nothing. The rule that came out of it — never quote throughput without its acceptance rate and prefill fraction — is why every row in the ledgers below carries them.

The three instruments

Three measurements decide most of what follows. Span timers bracket the three phases of a speculative step — drafter, verify forward, committer — on the GPU, which localizes a cost to a phase. A per-step regression records every individual step with its own draft count, then fits step = F + m × eps to separate what a step costs to exist from what each additional speculative event costs; this is the instrument that produced the campaign's structural result, and it replaced an arm-level fit that was wrong in both directions. Kernel profiles attribute time inside a phase to specific kernels, and are the only instrument that can distinguish real work from a gap.

Two properties of these instruments turned out to matter as much as their readings. They must be placed on the code path the deployment actually takes — one committer timer in this series was wrapping a function the live path never called — and they must not perturb what they measure: a diagnostic that copied per-row data to the host inside the span it was timing inflated that span from 42 to 106 milliseconds, and its arm's speed numbers were struck.

The truncated draft head — and why it had been refuted before

Observation. The drafter's GPU time was dominated by weight traffic, and its largest single read is the output projection over the full vocabulary — paid once per drafted level.

Mechanism. Draft from a gathered subset of the output head — 64k entries rather than the full vocabulary — so the drafter reads roughly half the bytes. The verifier keeps the full head, so every committed token is still scored against the true distribution and the result is lossless by construction. A draft token the truncated head cannot propose is simply a draft that does not get proposed; it is never a token that gets committed wrongly.

Effect and evidence. Drafter GPU time fell from 94.9 to 56.3 ms/step. The quality question — does a smaller draft head propose worse drafts? — was settled with a matched control on the same workload: acceptance was 5.552 with the truncated head against 5.556 with the full head. The reason it is free is measurable: the 64k gather covers 97.1% of the draft ids actually used, at half the read.

Why this is the campaign's most instructive success. Vol. VII reported this exact lever refuted. It had been ranked the top drafter lever on a bytes argument, then killed by measurement: the drafter was host-orchestration-bound, so halving its largest weight read bought about two percent of a wall dominated by launch glue. That verdict was correct at the time and stopped being correct the moment drafter graph capture removed the host cost. With the glue gone, what remained was actual weight traffic, and the original arithmetic became true. A refutation carries an implicit clause — while this other thing dominates — and because we had recorded the reasoning alongside the verdict, revisiting it was cheap.

The subtree-parallel scan — the one structural change

Observation. The recurrent scan kernel processed all twenty-two tree nodes serially inside every program. Its only parallel axes were over heads and value tiles; the node axis was sequential, because a recurrent scan is inherently sequential.

Mechanism. A scan is sequential along a path. A tree is only sequential along each path — after the shared prefix, its disjoint subtrees have no data dependence on one another whatsoever. So partition the nodes at runtime from the parent array into a prefix plus K independent subtrees, scan the prefix in one phase, then scan the subtrees concurrently on an added program axis, each still serial internally. The critical path drops from the node count to roughly the prefix plus the deepest subtree, and the saving grows with wider trees — which is the direction the acceptance work pushes.

Effect and evidence. Byte-exactness was the first question, and it has a clean argument: per-node arithmetic is unchanged and the order within each path is unchanged, so the floating-point chains are identical. That was confirmed on a self-check gate, then in a capture arm. Speed measured +4.7% at batch one. The more interesting number came later, from a window-matched live measurement on real tasks: the decomposition itself is worth −22.3 ms per event, while the captured side-stream topology it requires carries a +32.7 ms fixed cost per step. Those cross over at about 1.47 events per step — well below where we operate — so the lever is a clear net win at the operating point, and the measurement's explicit conclusion was to leave it baked.

That fixed component prompted its own follow-up attempt, which failed instructively and appears in the next section.

The committer sequence — three levers on one span

Observation. Vol. VII localized most of the tree's deficit to the committer, and left it at roughly 17 ms/event after dropping a redundant scratch-zeroing. Span timers showed the remainder was structure, not arithmetic: forty-eight per-layer calls, host-side assembly of the replay inputs, and host synchronizations inside the re-linearizer.

Mechanism and effect, in sequence. Batching all forty-eight layers into one pass took the span from 47 to 36 ms with the verify forward unchanged — the control that matters, since a lever that moves an untouched span is measuring phase, not itself. Staging the replay ring inside the kernel instead of assembling it host-side took the board from 32.85 to 38.01 tok/s at accept 4.520. Removing the host synchronizations from the KV remap brought the committer to 12.33 ms/event with no errors.

Evidence. Each passed an offline byte gate before any live run — the batched committer's gate compared committed state across varying accepted lengths and batch sizes and returned all-identical — then a live agentic gate, in-band and garble-free. The committer that Vol. VII called the dominant term is no longer the dominant term.

The big one: capturing the whole speculative region

This attempt is worth its own account, because it consumed more of the campaign than any success in the previous section and because its failure mode is one we would not have predicted.

Observation. The speculative region — the rejection walk, the product assembly, the convolution update, the committer — executed as a long sequence of small kernel launches with host work interleaved. Capturing the whole region into a single CUDA graph would eliminate the per-launch overhead and the interleaved host work in one move. The arithmetic was attractive and the design was sound.

What it took. Thirty-five boots. The region had to be made capture-legal first, which meant hoisting host-dependent work out of it, replacing data-dependent control flow with device-side selects, and building a warmup capture for every key shape. Along the way it produced a genuine correctness milestone — the whole region captured, byte-identical, with the committer running in-graph — and it produced both of the silent bugs documented in the next section, since the warmup capture is exactly where the missing sampling constraints came from.

What it delivered on speed: nothing measurable. The honest instrument was a pooled regression across seven arms spanning every graph mode. All four modes land on one line — step = 235.5 + 49.2 × eps — with mode residuals inside ±1.6 ms. There is no detectable mode difference at all.

Why. The prerequisite ate the prize. To make the region capturable we had to hoist its host cost out first — and that host cost was the thing the capture was meant to eliminate. What remained inside the captured region was launch gaps, and launch gaps in this region are smaller than the instrument can resolve. The lever's projection had been computed against a cost that its own enabling work removed.

The near-miss worth recording. Before the pooled regression, arm-level numbers appeared to show a clean ranking of the modes — a “slope ladder” in which one mode looked 21% worse per event than another. It was an artifact: throughput-per-event is not normalized by acceptance, because step time itself grows with acceptance, and the arms had been running at different acceptance rates. Acceptance alone reproduces every arm's apparent slope. We had been about to attribute a real-looking 21% difference to a code path that does not cause it. The correction is recorded in the campaign log against our own earlier claim.

The lever class that only exists at batch one

Observation. Profiling a single request showed gaps — time when the GPU had nothing queued while the host prepared the next piece of work. Overlap is the standard answer, and a queue of designs existed to hide host work behind GPU work and to overlap the committer under the drafter.

What killed it. A profile at the actual deployment configuration — four concurrent requests, the shape we ship — showed the GPU already 97% busy, with the remaining idle segmented across steps rather than pooled into hideable gaps. There is nothing to overlap into. The same capture showed why the batch-one intuition misleads: most steps at deployment are mixed, with prefill and decode sharing weight passes, so a pure-decode model describes only the decode share of the wall.

The rule we adopted. Design at batch one, where mechanisms are legible and a single request's structure can be reasoned about — but price every lever by its share at the deployment point before building it. A millisecond saved in a gap that does not exist at concurrency is a millisecond of nothing, and we had a queue of such levers ready to build.

Two attempts killed before they were built

The cheapest two verdicts in the campaign are worth noting for their cost. Single-streaming the subtree scan was designed to remove the +32.7 ms fixed cost that the subtree lever brought with it — a clean idea with a real target. One source read killed it: the launch was already single-stream, so the fixed cost is the per-level launch structure, not stream overhead. Total cost, one file. Cutting the deep tail proposed dropping the retrieved suffix nodes to save drafter time, on the observation that their per-position acceptance is low. Attribution showed the tail carries 0.85–1.31 of the acceptance — low rate over many positions is not low contribution — making it an anti-lever. Both refutations cost less than an hour and would each have cost days to discover after implementation.

What It All Added Up To

The model: step = F + m × eps. Fit step time against speculative events per step, and the intercept F is what you pay for existing while the slope m is what you pay per event. Where your deficit sits decides what you optimize.

Why it matters: a marginal deficit gets worse as your speculator improves. A fixed deficit gets diluted. Same milliseconds, opposite strategy.

None of the sixteen attempts was aiming at this, but together they produced the campaign's most consequential result — visible only once enough of them had landed to re-fit the model. For six volumes we treated the tree's slowness as a property of branching — the committer replay, the wider verify, the per-node work — and every one of those is a per-event cost. That framing predicted something uncomfortable: the better the tree got at accepting, the more of these costs it would pay, so its acceptance advantage could never fully convert. Vol. VII's numbers were consistent with exactly that, and an earlier measurement had the tree paying roughly 140 ms per event against native's 49.

Fitting step time against speculative events per step, across seven tree arms spanning every graph mode we ship, gives a different answer:

The slopes are the result. The tree's per-event cost is +4.6 ms over native — against a confidence interval of [−15, +24], which spans zero. Statistically the two lines are parallel: one more accepted event costs the tree what it costs native. The historic ~140 ms/event row tax is not merely reduced, it is excluded by the interval. Whatever the tree still pays, it does not pay it per token.

The entire remaining deficit sits in the intercept, and it is large: about 75 ms/step on this fit, or — on the more robust reading our own audit prefers — a total deficit of 85–90 ms/step at the operating range, measured where the native data is direct rather than extrapolated. That leaves the tree at roughly 0.92× native where it actually runs.

Why the reframe changes the strategy

Throughput is committed tokens over step time, so with the deficit purely fixed the two ways to win fall out as arithmetic. Cut the fixed term — roughly 85 ms — and the tree reaches native at equal acceptance; the fixed term is not mysterious either, being mostly the drafter proposing a twenty-one-node tree where native proposes a five-token chain (~54 ms), the committer's staged span (~39 ms), and host publishes. Or raise acceptance: at the current fixed cost, an accept of 5.0 clears native at the operating point, because every additional token committed per event spreads the same toll over more output. We sit at 4.3–4.75.

That second condition is the one worth dwelling on, because it is the inversion. Under the old marginal-tax picture, the accept-beyond-5 work was a nice-to-have that the row tax would partly eat. Under a fixed-cost picture, acceptance is a direct win condition — the tree's one native advantage now converts cleanly, and every token of accept above the current rate is throughput we keep. The deep suffix tail of Vol. VII stopped being a curiosity and became the shortest path to beating native.

Two Silent Bugs

What makes these worth telling: neither crashed, neither tripped a gate, and both lived inside code that had been reviewed and shipped. One changed every number we published for seven weeks; the other made a captured graph quietly stop doing part of its job.

Two defects surfaced in this campaign that had been running silently for a long time. They are unrelated in mechanism but share a shape: a piece of configuration that was absent rather than wrong, in a place where absence had a plausible-looking effect instead of an error.

Bug one: seven weeks at the wrong temperature

Our patcher injects a sampling-constraint application into the speculative verify path. It had been written against an image whose stock rejection sampler did not apply constraints itself, and its guard checked only for the patcher's own copy — not for a stock one. When the stock file later grew its own application, the guard did not notice, and both fired. Applying a temperature scaling twice does not apply it twice as hard in a linear way; it squares it. A requested temperature of 0.6 became an effective 0.6² = 0.36, and had been so for every speculative arm since the fifth of June — roughly seven weeks of campaigns.

A temperature that is too low is a particularly quiet bug, because nothing breaks. The model simply samples more greedily than intended; drafts get easier to guess, acceptance drifts up, and every arm is wrong in the same direction, so A/B comparisons keep looking sane. There was even a subtler side effect: the second application landed asymmetrically, so parent rows were being evaluated at 0.36 while the tree's own rows saw 0.6 — an inconsistency inside a single arm that no gate was watching for.

The fix applies the constraint only when the stock path lacks it, with a patch-time escape hatch (FR13_TEMP_LEGACY_DOUBLE=1) that reproduces the old double-apply byte for byte — deliberately kept, because the honest way to re-base seven weeks of numbers is an A/B against the bug rather than a declaration. That A/B is reassuring: at true 0.6 the best arm accepts 4.331 against the 0.36 control's 4.434, a difference of about a tenth of a token. The comparisons the campaign made survive; the absolute regime they were made in does not, and every pre-fix number now carries the caveat.

Figure 2Two absences with plausible symptoms: a guard that checked the wrong thing, and a capture built from an empty config

Figure 2 shows both bugs as the same species. In (A) a guard tested for the wrong thing, so a constraint ran twice and squared a temperature — a change with no error and a plausible-looking effect. In (B) a graph was captured from a configuration whose constraints were unset, so the recorded graph simply had no constraint kernels in it; every replay afterwards sampled an unfiltered distribution at full speed. Neither failed loudly. Both were found only by measurements that could not be explained any other way.

Bug two: a captured graph that forgot to filter

The second bug is a CUDA-graph hazard worth generalizing. To capture the sampler into a graph we build a warmup step with a dummy sampling configuration. That dummy had top_k and top_p unset. A graph records the kernels that actually execute during capture — so with no constraints present, the captured graph contained no top-k or top-p kernels at all. Every subsequent live replay ran at full speed and sampled from the raw, unfiltered tail of the distribution.

The symptom was spectacular and, for a while, misleading: rare-token salad in the output, acceptance collapsing to about 1.9, and four of four tasks garbling. It looked like a state-corruption bug — exactly the family this series has chased since Vol. V — and the hunt went there first, through a long arc of paired-replay probes comparing a live replay against a staged rerun on the same inputs. Those probes produced a per-row misalignment signature, then an apparent exoneration that we later had to retract because only one arm's random state had been pinned.

What cracked it was pinning both sides. With the randomness identical on both arms, the walk products came out byte-identical while the emitted tokens still diverged. That combination is decisive: if the state and the walk and the random draws all agree and the tokens do not, the difference cannot be in the state, the walk, or the RNG — it has to be in the sampling distribution itself. From there the missing constraint kernels were a short step. The fix captures with real constraint tensors so the kernels are recorded, and refills their values from live metadata each step, because a graph bakes addresses rather than numbers. Garble went to zero, output returned to coherent English, and on the first clean boot the agent landed a genuine one-character fix in a real file with its tests passing.

The general lesson

Capture-time configuration is part of a graph's semantics. If a knob is unset when you capture, its kernel does not exist at replay — silently, and at full speed.

Family A — correct eliminations of a partly-phantom target.

The in-span idle that was largely a bench artifact

superseded

Why it looked right: the per-step regression showed roughly 28–30 ms per event of non-kernel time inside our own verify-span brackets. Reproducible over thousands of steps, large enough to matter, and it survived three eliminations — so it became the campaign's target.

  • What killed it: profiling on live tasks at realistic context showed the verify span is mostly real kernels. Attention alone runs 77 ms per draft at deployment context against 0.9 ms at the short contexts we had been profiling. The “idle” was substantially an artifact of the bench, and the achievable floor moved up.

Lesson: profile at the context length you deploy at. A short prompt makes attention nearly free, which inflates every fixed overhead's apparent share and can manufacture a gap that is not there.

Host preparation before launch

settled

Why it looked right: the natural explanation for non-kernel time inside a span is host work between the span opening and the launch — input preparation, metadata assembly.

  • What killed it: splitting the span into a host half and an execution half measured the host side at essentially zero. The idle sat entirely after enqueue.

Lesson: split the bracket before theorizing about what is inside it. One extra event answers what a week of hypotheses cannot.

A host tail after enqueue, and a silent piecewise fallback

settled

Why they looked right: if the idle was not before the launch it might be after it — a host tail folded into a late stop event. And a step that quietly fell back from a full captured graph to piecewise execution would carry exactly the kind of overhead no kernel table shows.

  • What killed them: a CPU-clock discriminator measured the tail at 0.4–0.5 ms against 267 ms of execution. And tagging every step with its dispatch mode returned full-graph on 6329 of 6329 steps — no fallbacks at all.

Lesson: make the system state its own mode on every step. “Is it silently doing the slow thing” is the cheapest question to answer definitively and the most expensive to leave open.

Family B — observer effects.

A debug rider that doubled its own span

contaminated

Why it looked right: an arm carrying a detailed per-row trace reported a committer span of 106 ms against a normal 42 — a dramatic and apparently urgent regression.

  • What killed it: the trace itself. Its per-row device-to-host copies were serializing the very span it was timing. The arm's correctness results stand; its speed numbers were struck from the record and re-measured on a clean arm.

Lesson: a diagnostic that touches the device inside the region it times is not measuring that region. Keep trace arms and speed arms separate, and mark trace-arm timings unquotable at the source.

The instrument that lost its own samples, twice

contaminated

Why it looked right: the per-step sampler was the right idea and eventually produced the campaign's central result. Its first two versions simply reported nothing, which reads like a null result rather than a broken tool.

  • What killed them: version one wrote into a file the harness rewrote every second, so a run with hundreds of timed steps reported zero samples; version two lost everything to the container teardown's kill signal. Only a separate sidecar with throttled dumps and a boundary-catching poller retained data.

Lesson: an instrument's persistence path is part of the instrument. Validate that it survives the harness's own lifecycle before believing a null reading from it.

A timer on a function the deploy path never called

contaminated

Why it looked right: the committer replay was timed around a real replay function, and the numbers were plausible enough to feed an entire investigation.

  • What killed it: that function was the batched sibling; the deployed path calls the singular per-layer variant through its own loop. Moving the timer into the one shared function both wrappers call — and dividing by the per-commit layer count — changed the attribution materially.

Lesson: verify the call graph before trusting a timer, especially when a same-named sibling exists. Instrument the function the deployment actually executes.

Family C — claims that outran their evidence.

The exoneration we retracted

contaminated

Why it looked right: during the garble hunt a paired-replay probe appeared to clear the captured replay path — its outputs differed from a staged rerun only in ways randomness could explain.

  • What killed it: only one arm's random state had been pinned. With both sides pinned the comparison inverted: identical draws, identical walk products, still-divergent tokens — which is precisely what localized the bug to the sampling distribution and found the missing constraint kernels.

Lesson: “the difference is explained by randomness” is admissible only when the randomness is pinned on both arms. A half-pinned comparison can exonerate the guilty.

Our own parity headline

contaminated

Why it looked right: the best arm measured 43.57 tok/s where the native fit predicts about 43.7 — parity, in a series that had never reached it, and an extremely quotable sentence.

  • What killed it: our own audit. The native comparand is an exact two-point line with zero degrees of freedom, whose slope is identified by a single batch-4-versus-batch-8 contrast in which batch size, task set, boot date, and prefill fraction all vary together — and one of the two points had three of its four tasks exit early. The tree side is a single arm at one prefill fraction.

Lesson: audit your best number before publishing it, not after. A fit with no residual degrees of freedom is not evidence, however precisely it passes through your data.

Reflection: What Sixteen Attempts Taught

Worth stating plainly: the single most valuable artifact this campaign produced is not a kernel. It is a per-step regression with real confidence intervals, and the discipline to let it retire our own earlier claims.

Sorting sixteen attempts into eight that paid and eight that did not, the sorting rule is unusually clean. Every lever that paid removed structure — host work, launch counts, synchronizations, serialization — and none of them changed any arithmetic. Every lever that missed either targeted a cost that had already moved, or targeted a gap that existed only in the regime we were profiling rather than the one we ship. That is a more useful heuristic than any individual result here: before building, ask whether the cost you are attacking is still where you measured it, and whether it exists at your deployment point.

The second lesson is about the shape of a wrong turn. Nearly every one in this volume came from a number that was real, reproducible, and about something other than what we thought. The graph-mode ladder was real and was about acceptance rates. The in-span idle was real and was substantially about context length. The batch-one gaps are real and are about batch one. None of these were sloppy measurements; each was a correct measurement of the wrong quantity, which is much harder to notice because it survives repetition.

The defense that actually worked was structural rather than careful: fit a model with a covariate in it, and report intervals. The moment step time was regressed against acceptance instead of compared across arms, the ladder dissolved, the pooled fit was exposed as wrong in both directions, and the fixed-versus-marginal split — the thing that reframed the whole project — fell out of the same fit. Intervals then did the second half of the job by telling us which of our own headlines to retire, including the most quotable one in this volume.

Threats to Validity

Honest scope: the robust claim is the marginal one — the row tax is dead, with an interval that excludes it. Nearly everything else in this volume is a point estimate with wide intervals, and we say so where it matters.

The native control

Two points, zero degrees of freedom

Our native line is an exact fit through two arms whose slope is identified by a single batch-4 versus batch-8 contrast, with batch size, task set, boot date, and prefill fraction all varying together — and one of the two points had three of four tasks exit early. Every native comparison in this volume, including the parity reading, inherits that weakness. A properly-powered native sweep is the single highest-value measurement we do not yet have.

Wide intervals

Point estimates doing heavy lifting

The fixed-deficit interval spans [32, 118] ms and the marginal difference spans [−15, +24] ms/event. “The deficit is fixed” is well supported at the level of which term carries it; the precise 85–90 ms figure is a working number, not a measured constant.

Single arms

Best-board results are one run each

The 43.57 arm, like most cells on the board, is a single run at one prefill fraction, on a four-task subset, with workload-phase effects unquantified. The campaign's band gate catches gross breakage, not run-to-run variance of a few percent.

Re-based history

Seven weeks of numbers carry a caveat

Every pre-fix absolute number was taken at an effective temperature of 0.36. The controlled A/B suggests arm-to-arm comparisons survive — accept moved about a tenth of a token — but any absolute figure from that period should be read as off-regime, and the new reference band starts after the fix.

Future Work

The frame: with the deficit fixed, every remaining lever is worth exactly its milliseconds divided by the step — and acceptance is worth more than it used to be, because it now dilutes the toll instead of multiplying it.

The two win conditions are arithmetic: cut roughly 85 ms of fixed per-step cost, or reach an acceptance of 5.0. Profiling at real context rebuilt the lever ranking around actual kernel cost rather than gap-hunting, and it puts the work in a clear order.

A new front: the tree is crowding out the prefix cache

The newest finding is not a speed lever at all, and it connects this work back to Vol. IV. The recurrent state pool is a fixed number of pages, and a speculative decoder must reserve enough of them to hold its in-flight state. A five-token chain reserves few; a twenty-one-node tree reserves many. At four concurrent requests the tree reserves 88 pages against native's 24 — and since the pool is finite, that leaves 39 cacheable checkpoints against native's 103.

In other words the tree does not only cost time per step; it costs prefix-cache capacity, which on an agentic workload full of repeated context is its own throughput term — one that none of the step-time analysis in this volume captures. Two ungated fixes exist for it, capping the node-bank reservation and the speculative block allocation, with a real trade-off against block size to be measured rather than assumed. The measure-first sequence is written; the measurement has not run. It is a reminder that a decoder's cost has more axes than its step time, and that the ones you are not instrumenting are the ones most likely to be paying for your wins.
