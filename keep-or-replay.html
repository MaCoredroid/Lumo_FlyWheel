Keep or Replay

A Campaign to Make a Branched Speculative Tree Earn Its Keep — the First Lossless Deep Suffix Decode, the Committer Cut Sixfold, and the Whole Gap Measured to the Millisecond

Vol. VI restored the superset, so the branched tree now accepts more tokens per forward than native linear decode. This volume is the campaign to turn that acceptance into speed — and it moved the ball a long way. Since the last post we shipped the first lossless Gated-DeltaNet tree suffix decoder past depth-5 (garble-free across forty-eight task-runs), instrumented the entire tree-versus-native step to the millisecond, and cut the tree's committer overhead roughly sixfold — from a supposed hundred-millisecond fundamental tax down toward native's own floor. The instrument that made all of it possible was a single, clean diagnosis: the whole tree-versus-native gap reduces to one asymmetry in how the two paths commit — native keeps the recurrent states it already computed; the tree, by branching, must replay the accepted path through forty-eight layers. Here is what we built, what we measured, and the honest, now-quantified distance that remains.

TL;DR: a large characterization-and-optimization campaign, with four concrete deliverables. (1) The first lossless GDN-tree suffix decode past depth-5 — clean accept 4.29, zero garble across forty-eight task-runs, a novel and correct result. (2) A per-component decomposition of the tree-versus-native step to the millisecond that localized ~78% of the gap to the committer and corrected a plausible wrong guess: the drafter is equal, not host-heavy. (3) The committer cut from ~100 to ~17 ms — a 43-millisecond scratch-zeroing was redundant and dropped, and the recurrent replay itself is only ~16 ms, near native's seven. (4) A dozen levers built and adjudicated, a break-even quantified, and an architectural path to parity or a ~10% win laid out. The honest status, stated plainly and not buried: native MTP-5 is still the throughput floor — the accept edge doesn't yet convert, and the millisecond the committer shed largely moved to the verify forward — but the gap is no longer mysterious. It is measured, mechanistic, and addressable.

Abstract

Speculative decoding pays for itself when the tokens it drafts are accepted often enough that the shared verify forward, amortized over several committed tokens, beats decoding one token at a time. A branched token-tree accepts more per forward than a linear chain, because it offers the verifier several candidate continuations instead of one — and across this series we made that tree lossless (Vol. III), cache-correct (Vol. IV–V), and, once its acceptance was no longer garble-inflated, a genuine superset that out-accepts its subsets (Vol. VI). This page reports the campaign to turn that acceptance into throughput, and it is, first, a large body of progress: we built the first lossless deep suffix decoder past depth-5, characterized the entire tree-versus-native step to the millisecond, and cut the tree's committer overhead roughly sixfold. Those same measurements produce a verdict, which we state plainly rather than bury — on this deployment workload native linear multi-token prediction is still the throughput floor — and for the first time we can say, to the millisecond, exactly why, and exactly what would change it.

The cause is an asymmetry in the committer — the step that, after a speculative forward, writes the accepted path's recurrent state so the next forward can continue from it. On this model, 48 of 64 layers are recurrent Gated-DeltaNet, so “the state” is a per-layer scan carry, not a slice of a KV cache. Native's accepted tokens form a linear chain, so it already computed and kept each token's carry and simply selects the accepted one — about seven milliseconds. The tree branches, and a branched verify deliberately does not export every node's carry (retaining all of them would run to gigabytes), so after acceptance the tree must replay the accepted root-to-leaf path through all 48 layers to reconstruct its carry — on the order of a hundred milliseconds, and squarely on the critical path, since the next forward cannot start until that state exists. A per-component GPU-timer decomposition puts that replay at roughly 78% of the entire tree-versus-native step-time gap; the verify forward is ~18%, and the drafter — contrary to an earlier hypothesis this work retracts — is equal.

We then spent the campaign attacking the committer and reporting what moved. A 43-millisecond scratch-zeroing (“burn”) that the served path never reads was proven redundant and dropped; a CUDA-graph capture cut the replay's dispatch cost more than fivefold, byte-identically; batched gathers replaced forty-eight per-layer host round-trips with a handful. Those brought the committer from ~100 toward ~53 milliseconds — and then a narrower timer re-attributed the remainder: the GDN replay proper is only ~16 milliseconds, near native's seven, while a previously-hidden ~37–41-millisecond sampling decision is the larger cost and the real next target. A break-even model ties it together: given the measured step times, the tree overtakes native only above an acceptance of roughly 5.76 tokens per event, and its honest, garble-free acceptance is 4.29 — the earlier ~5.2 figures were repeat-loop artifacts from a period when two “baked” correctness flags were silently defaulting off. The deliverable is therefore a lossless branched-tree recurrent-state committer with deep suffix decode past depth-5 — a correctness milestone — and an honest verdict that native MTP-5 is the throughput floor, closable only by architecture, not by cheap levers.

The governing asymmetry (keep vs replay): after a speculative commit, the next forward needs the accepted path's recurrent carry. A linear accept lets you keep every candidate's carry and select the accepted one, because the candidates are the chain itself. A branched accept cannot — the tree computed many nodes' carries in a fused kernel that discards them rather than pay to export all of them — so it must recompute the accepted path. Keeping is O(1) selection; replaying is a 48-layer scan. That gap is not a bug and not a tuning knob; it is the price of branching on a recurrent model, and it is what this page measures, attacks, and bounds.

Contribution

One measurement and one mechanism, and the discipline to report a negative. The measurement is a per-component GPU decomposition of the tree-versus-native step that localizes ~78% of the gap to the committer and, crucially, corrects a plausible wrong story (the drafter is equal, not the culprit). The mechanism is keep vs replay: branching on a recurrent model forces a scan replay that a linear chain avoids by construction, on the critical path where nothing can hide it.

This page is the sequel to Vol. VI, whose restored superset made the throughput question finally meaningful, and is self-contained. Like the prior volumes it is a research report: it keeps the committer optimization ladder attempt by attempt, a full failure museum — a whole overlap scheme built and deleted, four committer levers refuted or marginal, and a methodology bug in which two “baked” flags were off for weeks — and an honest break-even that says what acceptance the tree would need and does not have. The future work is the only thing that closes the gap, and it is architectural.

What We Built: tail6, and Decoding Past Depth-5

The headline deliverable: tail6 is the first lossless Gated-DeltaNet tree suffix decoder to accept tokens past depth-5 — past the reach of the model's five multi-token-prediction heads — and it does so garble-free.

Why three arms: to test “branching buys acceptance” fairly, the tree is measured against native at its own depth, not only the shallow default. That control changes what the numbers mean.

Before the throughput story, the thing itself. The model drafts with five multi-token-prediction heads, so a plain linear draft — native MTP-5 — proposes a five-token chain and stops. The tree of this series does two things that chain cannot. Shallowly, at depths 0 through 4, it drafts a comb: the spine token plus two branch alternatives at each level, from the heads' runners-up, so the verifier can accept a near-miss it would otherwise reject. Deeply, at depths 5 through 10, it grows an Arctic suffix tail — a retrieved continuation from the request's own history, filling positions the five heads cannot reach. Together that is tail6: a twenty-one-node tree over an eleven-deep spine, and the first version of this stack to commit accepted tokens beyond depth-5, losslessly.

Figure 1tail6 is a depth-11 spine with a shallow MTP comb and a deep Arctic tail — measured against linear chains of 5 and 11 tokens

Figure 1 is what the three arms are. Native MTP-5 (A) is the shipped five-token chain. Native MTP-11 (B) is the fair depth-matched control — a linear chain drafted to the tree's own depth, no branching. tail6 (C) is the twenty-one-node tree: the shallow comb where the five heads offer alternatives, and the deep green Arctic tail where retrieval carries the draft past depth-5. The comparison this volume makes is against B as much as A — because a chain drafted just as deep is the honest test of whether branching earns its cost.

That tail is a real result. Reaching accepted tokens beyond depth-5 on a recurrent hybrid, losslessly and without garble, is something no earlier volume did — the five heads simply run out, and everything past them has to come from retrieval verified by the committer. On the clean run the tail engages exactly as designed: at the shallow depths the comb out-accepts native's chain outright, and the green tail keeps accepting where native MTP-5 has already stopped. The honest caveat, developed in the break-even section, is that the deep tail accepts at roughly half the rate of a trained head at that depth — a retrieved guess is weaker than a learned prediction — so the tail lengthens the draft without matching a same-depth chain token for token. But it exists, it is correct, and it is the substrate every throughput lever below is trying to make pay.

Keep vs Replay

The committer's job: after a speculative forward, the accepted path's recurrent state has to exist before the next forward can start — the next scan reads it as its initial carry. How you produce it is the whole story.

The machine: GB10 is a bandwidth-bound part — 273 GB/s of unified LPDDR5X, a ~99 ms weight-read floor per forward. On such a machine a small, occupancy-bound kernel that cannot fill the SMs is expensive out of all proportion to its FLOPs.

On this model 48 of 64 layers are recurrent Gated-DeltaNet, so “the state” the next step needs is not a slice of a KV cache — it is a per-layer scan carry, threaded token by token. After a speculative step commits, the next forward must begin its scan from the accepted path's carry. Native and the tree produce that carry in fundamentally different ways, and the difference is the entire throughput story.

Native keeps. Native MTP-5 drafts a linear chain of five tokens, and a chain advances the recurrent state contiguously: the accepted-path carry is computed inside the verify forward's own fused scan, which writes each token's final carry to its linear column. When acceptance lands at length k, the committed state is already sitting in column k−1; committing is an index select, not a computation. The mamba block simply advances linearly by the number accepted, with no per-token replay. Measured, native's committer is about seven milliseconds — the cost of a gather.

The tree cannot. A branched verify scores all of the tree's nodes in parallel, but which leaf is accepted is not known until after the committer's rejection walk decides it — after the forward is over. To keep states the way native does, the kernel would have to export every node's carry, for every layer, to memory, and then gather the one accepted leaf. That is not a little expensive; it is ~13.7 GB per step (about 13.6 MB per layer, times twenty-one nodes, times forty-eight layers), so the verify kernel deliberately does not export per-node states at all — it keeps only the cheap activation rings and reconstructs what it needs. Passing it a state buffer is a wiring error the kernel refuses. So after acceptance the tree must replay: re-execute the accepted root-to-leaf path through all forty-eight recurrent layers to rebuild its carry.

Figure 2A linear accept leaves its state in place to select; a branched accept threw its states away and must recompute the path

Figure 2 is the asymmetry. Native's verify scan writes every token's carry to its column as it goes, so the accepted carry is already there to select. The tree's verify cannot keep every node's carry — it would run to gigabytes — and the accepted leaf is not even known until the rejection walk finishes, so the tree must replay the accepted path through all forty-eight layers to rebuild it. Keeping is a gather; replaying is a scan. That is the price of branching on a recurrent model.

The replay is not slow because of launch overhead; it is slow because of occupancy. It is forty-eight tiny per-layer kernels, and each pins roughly one thread block per SM — the register-resident state tile is large enough that only one fits — so there is no co-resident warp to hide memory latency behind. Two natural ways to fix that were measured and both made it worse: running the forty-eight layers on concurrent streams (91.6 ms versus 76.6 ms serial) and fusing them into one batched launch (111.9 ms versus 76.5 ms), because the wall is occupancy and bandwidth, not the number of launches. The refutations are in the museum; the point here is that the replay is a floor, not a missed optimization.

And it is squarely on the critical path. The replay produces the exact state the next forward's scan reads as its initial carry, so the sequence verify → commit (replay) → next forward is a data dependency, not host slack. Asynchronous scheduling — which does help, overlapping the drafter's host work behind GPU time — cannot reorder the replay away, because nothing in the next forward can begin without its output. This is the deep reason native's committer is seven milliseconds and the tree's is not: native folds the accepted-path advance into the forward, for free on a bandwidth-bound machine, precisely because a linear accept lets it commit before the forward ends. The tree, by deferring “which leaf” past the forward, forfeits that fold. Everything the rest of this page does is either pay the replay down or try to earn it back in acceptance.

Where the Step Time Goes

The method: per-call GPU-timer spans around the three components of a speculative step — drafter, verify forward, committer — measured in the same run for both arms, so the comparison is same-boot and same-workload, not cross-run arithmetic.

The correction: this decomposition overturned our own prior guess. We had blamed a “host-heavy tree drafter.” The timers say the drafter is equal; the committer is the story.

A speculative decode step is three things: the drafter proposes candidate tokens, the verify forward scores them in one pass, and the committer writes the accepted path's state for the next step. Instrumenting each with its own GPU-timer span, in one same-boot run, decomposes the tree-versus-native step-time gap exactly. This is the diagnostic decomposition — taken before the committer was optimized, with the scratch-zeroing still on — and it is what aimed the campaign:

The Committer Ladder

The campaign: the diagnosis put ~78% of the gap on the committer, so we spent the campaign attacking it and reporting what moved — and, honestly, most of what looked like the tax turned out to be either redundant or misattributed.

The punchline: the committer was largely paid down — from ~100 to ~17 milliseconds — and native still won, because the cost moved rather than vanished.

The ~100-millisecond committer was not one thing. Decomposed, it was a large scratch-zeroing, the recurrent replay itself, forty-eight per-layer host dispatches, and the layout gathers that feed them. Attacking each in turn both cut the number and, more usefully, corrected what the number was.

The 43 milliseconds that did nothing

The single largest piece was not the replay. It was the burn: after each commit the tree zeroed its node-bank scratch — an eight-gigabyte fp32 region — a tree-specific tax native has no analog for, measured at 43 milliseconds, about 87% of what remained once the replay loop was graph-reduced. And it was redundant. Every cross-step reader of the recurrent state bank targets column 0, the request's running row, under the locked stateless-tree lifecycle; the burned rows are columns 1 and up, the drafted tree nodes, which nothing reads on the next step. A red-team pass confirmed the served path is byte-identical with the burn off, and it was dropped — carefully, behind the lifecycle's tri-flag guard rather than a bare deletion, because the burn stays load-bearing on a legacy path and a handful of default-off diagnostics do read the stale rows. That one change took the committer from ~80–88 down to ~53–57 milliseconds, for free.

The rest, batched and graph-captured — and a measurement that lied

The remaining machinery was host-bound in the ordinary way: forty-eight per-layer dispatches, ninety-odd per-layer host syncs, gathers done once per layer. Batching them — one layout conversion and four batched gathers in place of forty-eight round-trips — is byte-identical and removes tens of milliseconds of host time; capturing the per-layer scan loop into a single CUDA graph is also byte-identical (enabled by a state-neutral padding that leaves the recurrent state exactly unchanged) and cuts its dispatch more than fivefold. In the micro-benchmark the batched and graph variants both land near six milliseconds — native's own floor — so the graph was not even needed over the simpler batched path.

Then the live number refused to match the micro-benchmark: burn off, the committer still read ~53–57 milliseconds live against ~15 in isolation. The timer was lying — it wrapped the entire rejection-sampler dispatch, not the replay. A narrow timer, placed inside the one shared replay function the deployed path actually calls, settled it: the recurrent replay proper is 16.2 milliseconds, near native's seven. The other ~37–41 milliseconds is not replay at all — it is the sampling decision, the rejection walk that scores the tree's candidate paths and picks the accepted leaf, a cost that scales with branching and had been hiding inside the committer's timer the whole time. It is newly isolated, larger than the replay, and the real next target.

Break-Even, and the Accept That Wasn't There

The point of a model: throughput is acceptance over step time. Once the per-event step time is measured, you can compute the exact acceptance the tree would need to overtake native — and check it against what the tree actually accepts.

The twist: the acceptance the tree was thought to have (~5.2) turned out to be a garble artifact. The honest number is 4.29, and it moves the tree further from break-even, not closer.

With the committer paid down and the components measured, the break-even is a division. The tree overtakes native when its accepted tokens per event, over its per-event step time, beat native's — that is, when accept_tree / 160.9 > 3.422 / 103.5, so accept_tree > 3.422 × 160.9/103.5 ≈ 5.3. The tree accepts 4.29. It is a token below the bar, and the bar is set by a per-event cost the accept advantage has to pay back in full. (The earlier, rougher model — with the un-optimized committer inflating the tree step to ~177 ms — put break-even near 5.76; the paid-down committer lowers it to ~5.3, and the tree still does not clear it.)

The acceptance number itself needed correcting, and the correction cuts against the tree. For weeks, this exact tree geometry was reported at accept ~5.2 — tantalizingly near break-even. Those figures were garble artifacts. Two “baked” correctness flags — the attention-KV remap and the contiguous-spine reorder of the last two volumes — were hardcoded on only in a narrow locked launcher and were silently defaulting off in the general campaign launcher, so the campaigns ran without them. The result was degenerate repeat-loops (one task emitted 126 consecutive identical tool calls), and a repeat-loop is trivially predictable, so it inflates acceptance — a garbled window measured 40–124% higher deep-tail acceptance than a healthy one. With the flags actually on, the clean run has zero repeat-loops across all forty-eight task-runs, and the honest acceptance for this tail geometry is 4.286. The tree was never as close to break-even as it looked; the appearance of closeness was the same bug this series has been chasing since Vol. V.

Results: The Depth-Matched Three-Arm Run

The bar: a clean, garble-free, cache-on run at deployment config, with a depth-matched linear control so “the tree accepts more” is tested against a chain of the same draft depth, not just the shallow default.

Why depth-matched matters: the tree's justification is that branching buys acceptance. The fair control is not only native MTP-5 but an MTP-11 chain of the tree's own depth — and it changes the conclusion.

The verdict rests on one clean run: three arms, all cache-on, batch-4, temperature 0.6, nudge-free, on the same sixteen SWE-Verified tasks, after the two correctness flags were fixed to actually be on (see the museum for why that mattered). The arms are native MTP-5 (the shipped bar), the tree with its deep suffix tail, and a depth-matched native MTP-11 — a linear chain of the tree's own draft depth, so “branching buys acceptance” is tested against a chain that drafts just as deep.

Three readings, in order of how much they hurt the tree's case. One caution kept from the campaign: decode TPS here is the prefill-independent per-decode-step rate, the fair cross-arm number. It is not a verify-only ceiling — the trap of a forward-limited metric, which flatters the tree by charging only the shared forward and omitting the committer — and it is not a raw wall clock that a differing prefill fraction would confound. On the verify-only ceiling the tree looks far closer to native (a ~26% gap rather than the true ~50% full-step gap); this page reports the full-step number, because the committer the ceiling omits is precisely the term that decides the race.

Failure Museum

The recurring adversary was the harness, not the hardware. Baked flags that were off, a metric inflated by garble, a timer on a function the deploy path never called, a compile mistaken for a hang, a confounded baseline — as much of the campaign went to trusting the measurement as to beating the machine.

Eleven refuted levers and instructive failures, in three families: the measurement-hygiene failures that made numbers lie; the cheap committer levers that the replay's hardware floor refuted one by one; and the piggyback line — the architecturally-correct bet that was built, baked, and then deleted when its deep tail collapsed.

Family A — the harness was the adversary.

Two “baked” fixes that were silently off for weeks

contaminated

Why it looked right: the attention-KV remap and the contiguous-spine reorder of the last two volumes were both proven, and hardcoded on in the narrow locked launcher and a dozen diagnostics — so the docs said “baked,” and every campaign was assumed to inherit them.

  • What killed it: the general B4 campaign launcher — the one actually run — defaulted both flags off. Weeks of tree campaigns ran without the fixes; it surfaced only when two runs showed severe repeat-loops (one task at 126 consecutive identical tool calls). Fixed with a single source-of-truth flag file sourced by all three launchers plus a fail-loud boot assertion; the clean rerun then had 0 of 16 repeat-loops.

Lesson: “baked into the locked script” is not “baked into the launcher you actually run” — a flag proven on in one entry point is worthless if the production entry point defaults it off. One source of truth and a boot assertion, or it rots silently.

The accept~5 headline was a garble artifact

contaminated

Why it looked right: the tree's signature achievement was “accept > 5” — the first lossless suffix decode past depth-5 — and run after run showed deep-tail acceptance near five, reading as a genuine model-quality win.

  • What killed it: once the flags-off bug surfaced, the near-5 numbers were re-examined. A window dominated by a garbled task showed deep-tail acceptance 40–124% higher than a healthy window in the same run — a repeat-loop is trivially self-predictable, so the drafter “accepts” its own corruption and inflates the metric. The first clean, garble-free accept for this geometry is 4.286, not ~5.3.

Lesson: a degenerate or looping trajectory inflates acceptance, because repetition is trivially predictable. Never trust an accept number without first proving the run was garble-free.

The committer timer wrapped a function the deploy path never calls

contaminated

Why it looked right: the committer replay was timed around a real replay function; the numbers were plausible and fed the whole “replay is 78% of the gap” investigation.

  • What killed it: that function was the batched sibling; the deployed path calls the singular per-layer replay through the patcher's own loop. Moving the timer into the one shared function both wrappers call — and dividing by the forty-eight per-commit layer calls — isolated the true replay at 16 ms and revealed the rest was the sampling decision.

Lesson: instrument the function the deploy path actually calls, not a same-named sibling; verify the call graph, and account for per-layer versus per-commit call counts.

A ten-minute compile mistaken for a hang

settled

Why it looked right: one flag's boots sat unresponsive at capture-onset with no progress — the classic hung-flag signature — and the first-exercised permutation was the obvious suspect.

  • What killed it: a sampling profiler localized the “hang” to a Triton recompile of the GDN kernel — the intrinsic ~10-minute compile — and the no-flag control had the same ~9.5-minute gap. The flag was fine and was being killed prematurely.

Lesson: before declaring a hang, profile it and compare against the no-flag control's boot time. An O(N²) kernel recompile looks exactly like a deadlock.

A trimmable verify-gather, and a confounded baseline

contaminated

Why they looked right: the verify forward's ancestor gather looked like a losslessly-trimmable ~29% slice; and a run appeared to show a +9.5–12.7% accept improvement over a 4.317 baseline.

  • What killed them: the gather had already been made linear and measured only −1.7% — the +36 ms verify cost is inherent tree-size compute, not opportunity. And the 4.317 baseline was itself piggyback-confounded; the real baseline is 4.953, against which the “improvement” is roughly flat.

Lesson: a lever already optimized is inherent cost, not opportunity; and a delta over a confounded baseline is a mirage. Confirm the baseline is clean and a matched pair exists before claiming a win.

Family B — the replay is a hardware floor.

Copy-not-replay, killed on a napkin (13.7 GB)

settled

Why it looked right: if the replay is the cost, why replay? Native is cheap because it keeps its linear states and selects. So keep all the tree's per-node states and copy the accepted leaf directly — no recompute.

  • What killed it: exporting all twenty-one nodes' per-layer states is ~13.7 GB per step — infeasible on unified memory. The branching topology is exactly why you cannot keep all states; that is why the replay exists. Refuted before any build.

Lesson: the cheapest refutation is arithmetic. Size the memory of “just keep everything” before designing the copy path; branching-factor times state-size is often an instant no.

Overlap it, fuse it, swap the kernel — three refutations

settled

Why it looked right: the replay is forty-eight tiny per-layer kernels running at ~14× their bandwidth floor — clearly latency-bound — so overlapping them on streams, fusing them into one launch, or swapping in native's kernel all looked like easy ~60 ms wins.

  • What killed them, all measured: multistream was slower (91.6 vs 76.6 ms) — one replay grid already saturates the SMs, so the streams serialize on shared bandwidth. Batched-fused was slower (111.9 ms) on strided cross-bank indirection. Native-kernel replay was identical (77.3 ≈ 76.6 ms) — the cost is latency, not the kernel. And spine-commit, which skips the replay on a linear-prefix accept, only fires 53% of the time and pays a per-forward export, netting ~+15 ms — marginal.

Lesson: for a latency- and bandwidth-bound kernel on unified memory, “overlap / fuse / swap” buys nothing — there are no spare SMs or bandwidth to hide behind. The floor is architectural; only eliminating the work can move it.

Family C — the piggyback line: the right idea, built and deleted.

Piggyback: fold the replay into the forward

superseded

Why it looked right: the one lever the attack math endorsed. Instead of replaying, have the committer just record the accepted tokens and let the next forward's fused scan advance the state through [accepted path] ++ [new tree] — native's own trick. The state carry was provably reproduced to 1.19e-7, the variable-length padding was byte-exact, a 24-edit bundle landed, and it was baked on an “accept-neutral” basis (resolve 9/16, committer collapsed, 27.8 wall TPS).

  • What killed it: the “accept-neutral” bake was confounded. On deep-tail-heavy tasks accept collapsed ~1.5 tokens (~3.65 vs ~5.12), reproducibly. The fold seeded the subtree from a different kernel than native's, and its sub-ULP-per-node drift amplified into the arctic tail. Every fix fell short, and the entire line was deleted per explicit directive — seventeen cleanup commits, ~4,077 deletions.

Lesson: a byte-exact state carry does not guarantee a byte-exact output — a provably-correct state seeded a different-kernel path whose drift amplified downstream. Don't bake on an accept-neutral gate that hasn't been stress-tested where accept actually lives: the deep tail.

SCAN_ALIGN: match native's precision, backwards

superseded

Why it looked right: the piggyback tail collapse was narrowed to a seed mismatch — the fold rooted the subtree with the custom tree kernel while the reference used native's. Aligning the tree kernel's arithmetic to native's was proposed as “Fix A” and briefly declared the crack.

  • What killed it: it aligned the wrong way. The fold already carried fp32; forcing native's bf16 round-trip added drift, not removed it — echoing an earlier finding where the same alignment made garble worse. The collapse was reclassified as amplified within-floor drift, not a fixable seed.

Lesson: “match the other kernel's precision” cuts both ways — aligning a higher-precision path down to a lower one adds error. Confirm which side is more precise before aligning.

Base-column reorder: fixed the head, whiffed the tail

superseded

Why it looked right: the piggyback chain pushed the base subtree into different score-tile columns — exactly the reduction-reassociation class Vol. VI's spine reorder fixed — so a base-first permute should restore the base logits and lift the whole collapse. The head evidence supported it.

  • What killed it: the head fix worked exactly as designed and closed only ~5–20% of the gap (+0.08 to +0.36 against a ~4.9–5.7 target). The deep tail still collapsed. The −1.5 was not mostly a shallow tie amplified through the suffix; it was a largely separate deep-node verify effect.

Lesson: confirming a mechanism's direction is not confirming its magnitude. A plausible “shallow tie wrecks the tail” story predicted full recovery; the fix moved a fraction. Measure the total, don't infer it from the mechanism.

The ×14,800 amplification chase

superseded

Why it looked right: a per-layer drift ladder grew ~14,800× over depth — a dramatic, smooth, log-linear instability fingerprint — seemingly explaining how a one-ULP seed flips a token, and motivating a whole class of amplification-reduction levers.

  • What killed it: two corrections under adversarial verification. The ~14,800× was inflated ~30× by ordinary residual-stream norm growth that native shares, leaving genuine error amplification at ~492×. And that amplifier is the frozen trained Jacobian — shared bit-for-bit with native, so reducing it lowers a floor both arms sit on, not the gap. The differentiator is the seed, and you cannot lower a frozen Jacobian.

Lesson: before chasing an amplifier, subtract the part your baseline also has. A growth ladder inflated 30× and a Jacobian shared with the winning baseline mean the amplifier is a red herring — fix the seed.

Reflection: Measuring a Negative

Worth stating plainly: the result is that native wins, and the value is in why. A clean decomposition that says “the committer, because keep beats replay on a recurrent model” is worth more than a hero number, because it tells the next person exactly which architecture could win and which levers are dead.

Most of the campaign was not spent beating the hardware; it was spent learning to trust the measurement. Two flags proven on were off in the launcher that mattered; a signature accept number was inflated by the very garble those flags fixed; the committer's cost was timed on a function the deploy path never called; a baseline was confounded by an experimental arm; a compile looked like a hang. Each of these produced a confident, wrong number, and each was caught only by a control — a matched window, a no-flag boot, a call-graph check. The portable lesson of the whole volume is that on a mature, fast-moving system the harness is an adversary with the same standing as the GPU, and it earns the same adversarial verification.

Threats to Validity

Honest scope: the verdict's sign is solid — native wins in both the model and direct measurement, and the mechanism is measured. The magnitudes carry the usual caveats of a single deployment run.

Statistics

One clean run, sixteen tasks

The headline is one cache-on B4 run, one arm per cell, sixteen SWE-Verified tasks. Resolve and throughput track cleanly and all arms clear the ~28% base rate, but this is a directional deployment measurement, not a powered benchmark.

Metric basis

An open alignment flag at B>1

The live per-task speed reduction carries an alignment ratio near 0.53 between the GPU-derived and wall-clock throughput bases — a known artifact of mixing per-request and per-physical-step denominators at concurrency. Two candidate bases survive; the sign of the verdict holds under both, but the absolute tokens-per-second should be read as the compute basis it names.

Missing pair

No cache-on native GPU-timer capture

The cleanest native component breakdown is cache-off; a fully matched cache-on native-with-timers arm does not yet exist, so the native committer's ~2.5 ms is cross-referenced, not co-measured with the tree in every configuration.

Machine-specific

The economics are GB10's

The replay is expensive because GB10 is bandwidth-bound unified memory where an occupancy-starved kernel cannot be hidden. A machine with more SMs or HBM could change the replay's cost and, with it, the break-even — the verdict is for this hardware and workload.

Future Work: The Two Ways Under Break-Even

The prize, quantified: the attack math says eliminating the replay reaches parity (loses ~2%), and eliminating it plus trimming the verify tax makes the tree win by ~10%. That is the whole target — not a blowout, but a real win, and only reachable architecturally.

Throughput is acceptance over step time, so there are exactly two ways under the break-even line: make the step cheaper, or make the acceptance higher. The cheap committer-kernel levers are refuted (the museum); what remains is architectural.

The denominator: fold the replay into the forward

The durable win is to make the tree pay native's committer cost by doing what native does — advance the accepted-path state inside the next forward's fused, high-occupancy scan rather than in forty-eight separate latency-bound kernels. The committer would only record the accepted tokens; the next forward would take [accepted path] ++ [new tree] as input and thread the state through as part of its own scan, roughly free on a bandwidth-bound machine. That is the piggyback direction, and it is the one the attack math endorses — committer ~100 → ~16 ms, tree to parity, and to a ~10% win once the verify tax is trimmed. The specific implementation was built and deleted (its different-kernel seed collapsed the deep tail); the lesson it leaves is that the rebuild must seed the fold from the same kernel native uses, so the committed output, not merely the state, is faithful. It is the biggest rewrite on the board and the only path to a clear win.

The residual: the sampling decision

With the replay isolated at ~16 ms, the larger committer piece is now the ~37–41-millisecond sampling decision — the rejection walk that scores the tree's candidate paths and picks the accepted leaf, a cost that scales with branching and had been hiding inside the committer's timer. It is newly separated, not yet attacked, and has no design yet; it is the next thing to profile.

The verify tax, and the numerator

Two further levers are scoped. A verify trim targets the tree-attention tax directly — skipping the ghost query rows the kernel masks but still computes, and per-shape tiling — profiled first, aiming the verify forward from ~106 toward ~70 ms; together with a drafter graph-capture, the closeout projects the tree closing to native's throughput without touching acceptance. On the numerator side, accept-beyond-5 — a deep suffix tail and complement branches at a 32-node horizon that fits the same register budget — is designed, built, and lossless, and is the only route to clearing break-even by acceptance. Honestly, it does not clear it in-regime: the “accept > 5” readings were a repetitive-workload windfall, and the per-task average sits below the bar. A smart-matching drafter that reshapes the tree per step by the suffix-match signal — a deep chain when the match is long and confident, a wide comb otherwise — is designed and gated behind a kernel-shape probe. None of these is cheap, and the honest summary is that native MTP-5 is a strong floor: the tree's road back is a fused-replay rewrite, and even that aims for a win measured in single-digit-to-ten percent.
