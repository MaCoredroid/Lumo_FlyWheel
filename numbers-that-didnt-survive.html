Twenty-Seven Milliseconds, Shipped

A 7% Speedup That Was Invisible When We Tested It One Request At A Time — Why Serving Four At Once Changes Which Costs Matter, And What Happened When We Re-Checked The Rest Of The List

This volume ships two speedups on a system that generates text with a large language model. The first is live and credentialed: 27 milliseconds off every step when the server is handling four users at once, which is how it actually runs. The second is built and proven correct but not yet switched on: another 9 milliseconds. Together they are about 9% of the step. The interesting part is that the first one had already been rejected two weeks earlier as having no effect — because we tested it with a single user, and the thing it fixes only exists when the machine is busy. Chasing that down is what the rest of the volume is: a re-check of everything else we had concluded from single-user tests, which retired three of our four planned optimisations, and a correction to the yardstick we had been measuring all of it against. This page assumes no prior reading.

TL;DR: What got faster. Serving four concurrent requests, a step went from 408.3 to 381.3 milliseconds — 27.03 ms, or 6.6%. That is measured, sealed over four paired passes (95% lower bound +10.82 ms), gated at three successive commits, and is now the production default. On the paired timing run the same change reads −29.50 ms/step, −7.17%, with per-request throughput +6.04%. A second change — collapsing a two-pass recurrent scan into one — is built, proven byte-for-byte identical across 11,088 comparisons, and measured at a further −8.98 ms/step; it is off by default pending its final seal. Why it was nearly missed. Both changes measured roughly nothing when tested one request at a time. The reason is the core fact of this volume: a language model's weights get read from memory once per step no matter how many users share that step, so at four users the weight read is amortised and the costs that actually grow are the per-user ones — attention over each conversation's history, and each user's recurrent state. Those grow 3.3× going from one user to four while the weight-reading matrix multiplies grow 6%. A lever aimed at per-user work is invisible at one user by construction. What that forced. We re-derived the four biggest items on our own published optimisation list. Three were not there. A 10 ms "idle tail" was an average dragged up by 15 unusual steps out of 1146 (the real median is 3.6 ms). A 3.45 ms scan fusion was priced by a model that divided real time by work that never executes. A third was costed against a number that predated its own fix. We publish the defect in each. And the yardstick. We had been dividing a four-user step by a one-user floor. Corrected, the two operating points sit at 1.83× and 2.57× their respective physical floors — and per user, the four-user configuration is 1.81× closer to the floor than the single-user one, which is the opposite of what the uncorrected numbers said.

How This Works, From First Principles

If you already know how speculative decoding works, skip to the two operating points. Nothing later depends on reading this section, but everything later is easier with it.

Four facts explain every number in this volume. None of them requires knowing anything about our system in particular.

1. Generating one word means reading the entire model

The model here has 27 billion parameters stored in an 8-bit format — about 27 gigabytes of numbers. To produce a single token of output, the hardware has to read essentially all of them out of memory and multiply by them. There is no shortcut: every parameter participates.

The machine reads memory at 273 gigabytes per second. So one token costs at least 27 GB ÷ 273 GB/s ≈ 100 milliseconds of pure memory traffic, and with the extra prediction heads we run, 119.658 ms. That is not a software limit. It is the floor, and no amount of clever code goes below it.

This single fact is why text generation feels slow and why it is a memory bandwidth problem rather than a math problem. The chip's arithmetic units spend most of their time waiting.

2. Speculative decoding buys tokens in bulk

Here is the trick that makes it faster. If reading the weights costs the same whether you produce one token or check five, then guess five tokens ahead using something cheap, and verify all five in a single pass through the big model.

If the guesses were right, you got five tokens for the price of one weight read. If they were wrong, you throw them away and you have lost only the cheap guessing. A mathematical procedure called rejection sampling makes this exactly lossless — the output distribution is identical to not doing it at all. You are not trading quality for speed; you are trading wasted guesses for speed.

Our version guesses a branching tree rather than a straight line: instead of one five-token guess, several alternatives at each position. More guesses verified in the same single pass means more chances that one of them is right. We currently commit about 5.75 tokens per pass.

3. Serving several users at once shares the expensive part

Now put four users on the same server. Their requests can be processed in the same step — and here is the key consequence: the 27 GB weight read is shared. Read once, used for all four. So the most expensive single thing in the step does not get four times more expensive. In our measurements it grows by 6%.

But not everything is shared. Each user has their own conversation history that attention must look back over. Each has their own recurrent state to carry forward. Each needs their own output probabilities computed. All of that is per-user, and all of it is four times larger.

Figure 1The one idea behind this volume: what is shared and what is not

Figure 1 is the whole argument in one picture. The blue block is the mandatory weight read, and it is the same size whether the step serves one user or four. The amber block is everything that belongs to an individual user — their conversation history, their state, their probabilities — and it quadruples. A change that makes the amber block smaller barely moves the top bar and clearly moves the bottom one. We tested two such changes in the top bar, concluded they did nothing, and were wrong both times.

4. So the question "is this change faster?" has no single answer

It depends on how busy the server was when you asked. A change that shrinks per-user work will look like noise at one user and like a real win at four. A change that shrinks the shared weight read looks the same at both.

That is not a subtlety — it is the difference between shipping a 7% speedup and filing it as a null result, which is exactly what nearly happened here.

One term you will need It appears in almost every result below
width

How many requests were actually resident in a given step. "B4" is the configuration; width is what happened. A B4 run does not sit at width 4 the whole time — agents finish tasks, new ones start, and a real capture reads {width 1: 4 steps, width 2: 74, width 3: 236, width 4: 225}. Quoting one "batch four" number without conditioning on realised width averages four different physical regimes together, and doing that hid one of the results below.

the floor

The time the step would take if every mandatory byte moved exactly once at full memory speed, with zero latency, zero launch cost and perfect overlap. It is a lower bound, not a target. Nothing reaches it. It exists so that "is 381 ms good?" has an answer.

byte-identical

Our admission rule. A replacement kernel is only allowed to ship if its output numbers are bit-for-bit the same as the code it replaces, checked on real traffic. It is why speed claims here can be compared across months, and it is the constraint that makes several obvious optimisations illegal.

What Got Faster

Every number here is measured, not modelled. Where a figure comes from a model rather than a stopwatch, this volume says so — and the section after next is about what happens when you forget to.

Two changes. Both attack per-user work, which is why both needed four concurrent requests to show up at all.

Change 1 — pairing attention's query heads. Shipped.

Attention is the part of the model that looks back over the conversation so far. Internally it has 24 "query heads" that each read from a shared set of 4 key/value stores. That means every stored byte gets fetched multiple times — once per head that wants it.

The change re-maps which heads share which fetch, cutting the number of separate scans over each key/value store from six to three. Nothing about the arithmetic changes; the same numbers get multiplied in the same order. It is a data-movement change.

Change 2 — folding a two-pass scan into one. Built, proven, not yet armed.

The model carries a recurrent state forward — a running summary of the conversation that updates every token. Because we verify a tree of guesses rather than a line, updating that state means walking the tree, and we had been doing it in two passes: one for the trunk, one for the branches.

Two passes means the trunk's results have to be written out to memory and read back in by the branch pass. Measuring that handoff directly: 56.1% of the kernel's entire cost is moving state between the two passes, not computing anything. 46.0 µs of writes plus 82.3 µs of reads out of 228.74 µs per layer.

Doing it in one pass keeps the state in registers and deletes the handoff:

The Speedup That Was Invisible At One Request

The design in one line: serve the candidate at exactly one width, and every other width in the same run becomes a free control group.

Two weeks before the result above, the attention change was tested on a four-task screen and measured null. No effect. It was filed accordingly.

It was tested at one request at a time, against a version of attention roughly a third of its deployed size. From Figure 1, the reason is now obvious: the change shrinks per-user work, and at one user there is only one user's worth of it to shrink. The null was real, correctly measured, and irrelevant to production.

Re-tested at four requests, it is 7% faster. That is exactly the kind of reversal nobody should believe on one run — a single paired comparison on an agentic workload, where four independent agent sessions interleave differently every time. So here is the design that makes it credible, which is the most reusable thing in this volume.

The candidate serves at exactly one width

The new kernel is scoped so it only engages when four requests are genuinely resident. Every step at width 2 or width 3 — and there are hundreds of them in the same run — executes the identical original code on both arms.

Which means those narrower steps are a placebo group that costs nothing to collect. Same run, same machine, same four agents, same wall clock, same everything — and the treatment provably absent. If the "speedup" were really scheduling luck or a warmer cache or a friendlier task mix, it would show up at width 2 and width 3 as well.

And note the sign on the two controls: they both ran slightly against the candidate. Whatever run-to-run noise was present that day was working to make the candidate look worse, not better. So the raw 29.50 ms understates the effect. Correcting for it — comparing the treated width's change against the widest untreated control's change — gives 37.21 ms.

Three independent reasons to believe it, and one caveat that stands
A second instrument agrees

The original arm's own cost of going from three requests to four is +49.17 ms. A completely separate profiling run, attributing time kernel-by-kernel, put attention's four-request cost at +48.4 ms. Two unrelated instruments, 1.6% apart.

It is physically bounded

The change removes 76% of the cost of widening from three requests to four. The 37 ms sits inside the ~52 ms of headroom that attention was independently measured to have above its own theoretical floor. The result is possible, which is not always true of large deltas.

The old confounds are absent

The earlier null decomposed into two artifacts: guess-acceptance drifted 77%, and the candidate arm ran with 26.5% fewer co-resident requests. Here acceptance is flat (+1.16%) and residency is matched (−0.65%). Neither mechanism can produce this delta.

Stated plainly

This is one paired draw. The noise floor it clears was measured across four passes, which is not the same as the uncertainty of a single comparison — and the width-3 control shows 7.71 ms of arm-to-arm wobble all by itself. The placebo constrains where the effect is. It does not tell you the effect's precision.

The reducer now computes the placebo automatically and writes the verdict into the result file: if any untreated width gains as much as the treated one, it says so loudly instead of leaving a reader to notice. A control width needs at least 100 steps before it can move the correction, so a one-step blip cannot. And a code review caught the design's own footgun before it shipped — an earlier revision had let a treated width act as its own control, which would have quietly laundered the effect into its own baseline.

Re-Checking The Rest Of The List

Why this section exists: if a lever we had rejected turned out to be a 7% win, the reverse is equally possible. So we re-derived the biggest items we had accepted. Three of four were not there.

Ground rule: each re-derivation reproduces the original numbers exactly before disagreeing with them. Otherwise it is a different measurement, not a correction.

We had published a ranked list of what was left to optimise, priced against a single-request step. Its top items were worth 16.5 milliseconds between them. We built them.

Item 1 — the "10 millisecond idle tail" was an average of two different things

The claim: after the guessing phase finishes, the GPU sits idle for 10.08 ms while the host CPU does bookkeeping in Python. Move that bookkeeping off the critical path and recover most of it. We had called it "the single largest addressable block in the trace."

What it actually is: 3.46 ms. The median of that gap is 3.588 ms. The 11.977 ms mean is dragged up by 15 steps out of 1146 — 1.3% — where the gap is not a gap at all. Our profiling markers wrap only the guess-and-verify path, so when the server pauses to ingest a long new prompt, that entire operation lands between one step's marker and the next one's and gets counted as idle time. One of those was 3.06 seconds long.

And the fix would not have worked anyway. Reading what is actually inside the window — 154 GPU operations doing 0.209 ms of real work, plus about 45 short Python blocks — identifies it as the next step's input preparation. It is a precondition for the forward pass that follows it, not deferrable bookkeeping. "Move it off the critical path" has nothing it can move.

The same re-derivation caught a bigger error, and it was ours A published figure that was 617× too large
What we published

1.945 ms per step of GPU idle attributed to the cost of launching our big pre-recorded GPU command buffer. That figure also implicitly priced every future "reduce the node count" optimisation.

Measured directly

Launching that 1894-node buffer on the same hardware costs 3.15 microseconds. Not milliseconds. 0.443 nanoseconds per node plus 2.31 µs fixed.

The ratio

617×. The number was an artifact of the profiler itself — the tool's own overhead, attributed to the thing being measured. We had even written a caveat box saying profiler inflation "should not be quoted," without noticing it was already inside a headline.

What it now prices

Command-buffer node count is not a meaningful lever at all. A parked optimisation that removes 192 nodes per step is worth 0.085 microseconds.

Item 2 — the scan fusion was priced by dividing real time by imaginary work

The claim: the two-pass recurrent scan should be fused into one launch with fine-grained dependencies, worth 3.45 ms/step. We had been emphatic about the design detail — use per-node ready flags, not a barrier between the passes, because "that distinction is the 3.45 ms."

The distinction is worth zero, and the reason is a nice piece of scheduling. The tree's longest dependency chain is 12 nodes. The two-pass schedule's total length is the trunk (5) plus the longest branch (7) — also 12. It is already optimal. The barrier between the passes delays nothing, because the longest branch hangs off the last trunk node anyway; by the time the trunk is done, the critical path was going to start there regardless.

Figure 2Why the barrier is free: the critical path already runs through the end of the trunk

Figure 2. Removing a barrier only helps if something is waiting at it. Here the longest branch depends on the last trunk node, so it could not have started sooner even with perfect fine-grained dependencies. The short branches (dashed) do gain freedom — and they already had slack to spare. This is a case where the intuition ("fewer synchronisation points must be faster") is right in general and wrong for this particular graph.

Why 3.45 looked real is the more useful half, and it is an easy mistake to make. The cost model normalised by the kernel's padded capacity — 82 slots. Those slots never execute; the kernel's actual loop count is a per-branch length loaded at runtime. So the model was dividing real elapsed time by work that does not happen, producing a per-unit rate that was never achievable and then promising the difference. Tested rather than argued: forcing all eleven branches to genuinely run the padded length costs 401.89 µs against 158.30 µs for the real schedule.

But the same probe is where Change 2 came from. Looking for the fusion win, we measured the state handoff between the two passes and found it was 56.1% of the kernel. The lever was real. The model had just pointed at the wrong part of it.

Item 3 — a brief costed against a number that predated its own fix

The claim: roughly 340 GPU launches per step in the convolution commit path, ripe for batching.

What it is: one launch per event, and 0.542 ms/step — 0.2% of the step. The 340 figure was true when it was written and false by the time anyone acted on it; a fix had landed in between. The 48-layer Python loop the brief named is not on the served path at all, and a test already pinned that.

The 340 launches do exist, elsewhere: 528 per step of per-layer plumbing. But those live inside the pre-recorded command buffer where the measured cost is 213 nanoseconds per node, so collapsing all of them recovers at most 0.112 ms/step. What shipped instead is a small batching change worth 0.016 ms — and the commit message says exactly what that is worth: "400× below the measured noise floor... it ships because it is free and provable, not because it moves a gate."

The three mistakes, stated so you can avoid them None is exotic; all three are easy to repeat
Mistake 1

An average over two different populations

1.3% of samples were a different kind of event and carried a 3.3× inflation of the mean. Always report a median beside a per-step average, and look at what the extreme tail of the distribution actually is before you optimise against it.

Mistake 2

Dividing by work that never runs

Padded capacity is not executed work. A rate computed as real-time-over-padded-units is not a rate, and every model built on it will over-promise by exactly the padding factor.

Mistake 3

Costing against a superseded baseline

The number was true when written and false when acted on. A proposal should carry the commit its measurement was taken at, and be re-derived against current code before anyone starts building.

Worth being clear about the asymmetry here: killing a number needs only one demonstrated defect, so these refutations are better-evidenced than most of the claims they replace. That is a feature of negative results generally, and it is why they are cheap and worth doing early.

The Yardstick Was Measuring The Wrong Thing

Order matters: the correction that makes the four-request configuration look worse is applied first. A recalculation that only applies the flattering half is not a correction.

Every ratio in this series divides a measured step time by a hardware floor. The floors we had been using were wrong, and in a way that made the whole four-versus-one comparison meaningless.

The published comparison was: one request sits at 1.94× its floor, four requests sit at 3.0×, therefore the four-request case has far more room to improve. Three separate things are wrong with that, and they do not all point the same direction.

Correction 1 — wrong scenario, and it makes B4 look worse

The floor depends on which prediction heads are loaded. Our records publish several variants. The single-request numbers divided by 119.658 ms — the variant with small 64K-entry guessing heads. The four-request numbers divided by 126.514 ms — the variant with one full-vocabulary head.

But the four-request runs launch with the small head. Their own launch metadata says so, in three fields. They were being divided by a floor that includes 1.87 GB of weights they never read.

Put on the same basis, four requests is at 3.19×, not 3.01. Fixing this makes it look 6% worse — and that has to be done before the correction that makes it look better, or the second one is not credible.

Correction 2 — a weights-only floor is unfair to a busy server by construction

Go back to Figure 1. The floor we were using counted only the shared weight read. It explicitly excluded per-user traffic — the conversation history attention reads, the recurrent state carried forward, the output probabilities written and read back, the residual stream cycling through 64 layers.

That is exactly backwards for comparing batch sizes. The weight term is batch-invariant — and this is confirmed by measurement, not assumed: the big matrix multiplies grow 1.08× from one request to four, and the output head is flat above two. Meanwhile every excluded term is per-user and four times larger at four requests.

So a denominator built only from the invariant term flatters the single-request case and penalises the four-request case automatically. The honest floor applies the same rule to every term: mandatory unique bytes ÷ 273 GB/s, with the same conversation length assumed for both.

A kernel family nobody had named

Profiling reductions usually rank kernels by name and report the top ten. That method has a blind spot: a large number of individually small kernels never appears.

Opening the residual bucket that every previous reduction had left alone: 184 distinct kernels, firing 4,777 times per step, costing 83.92 ms. That is 19.5% of the step — the second-largest item after the matrix multiplies, and larger than attention. It had been sitting in a category called "other" for the whole campaign.

Two things inside it are worth naming. First, 9.2 ms/step of probability math runs on a fixed-size padded buffer regardless of how many users are resident — identical cost at width 2 and width 4, and absent entirely at width 1. At width 3, which is 44% of steps, a quarter of that work is provably wasted. Second, one 5.5-microsecond kernel fires 768 times per step.

And a twist worth recording: the 9.2 ms looked like the serving framework's sampler running a redundant generic path beside its own optimised one. It is not. Follow-up work found it is ours — our own tree-walking accept logic, doing 24 full-vocabulary softmaxes and cumulative sums per step on 4 of the machine's 48 processors. The kernels are generic library calls, so the natural assumption was that the library had called them.

Estimated recoverable: 33–62 ms/step, central ~47. Those bands are engineering judgement rather than measurement, published per class so a reader can reject one without discarding the rest. No candidate has been built. For scale, that band is two to four times the entire ranked lever list that had been built without opening the bucket.

Attention is not limited by memory bandwidth at four requests

Both of our attention floors rested on an assumption nobody had tested: that attention time is proportional to the conversation bytes it reads. The capture can test it, because when the server pauses to ingest a new prompt, a known number of bytes enters the cache and the steps on either side are clean.

An extra token costs attention at most 0.32 nanoseconds, against a 15.01 nanosecond memory floor — 47× cheaper than physics allows if it were reading that data from memory. So it is not reading it from memory. Proportionality is rejected.

The mechanism, and it is a satisfying one: attention uses 100 KB of on-chip scratch memory, which is the maximum, so exactly one thread block fits per processor and a "wave" is 48 blocks. At four requests the launch is 96 blocks — exactly two waves — and each wave contains all four users. A wave finishes when its slowest member finishes.

attention cost is set by   the longest conversation in the batch

not by   the total of all four

So attention's remaining ~63 ms of headroom is real, but it is a load-balancing problem, not a bandwidth one. If one user has a 40,000-token conversation and three have 5,000-token ones, all four pay the 40,000-token price. The fix is assigning work per-request rather than per-wave — a lever that has never appeared on any list in this series, and one that no layout change will substitute for.

Which makes this round's shipped attention change more interesting, not less: it was ranked on the bandwidth argument, that argument turns out to be false, and it still delivered 7%. The gain is real and the theory that predicted it was wrong — so the next attention lever should be aimed somewhere else.

Two Instruments That Were Lying

Theme of this section: a broken measurement is worse than no measurement, because it produces confident numbers. Both of these had been producing confident numbers for months.

The meter that had been recording nothing

Our accounting of how many tokens each campaign consumed had reconciled cleanly for months. On the four-request run it stopped: a gap of 189,780 tokens appeared, which looks exactly like requests leaking outside the ledger.

Request-level diagnosis found the opposite. All 639 of 639 engine requests were correctly attributed, with zero retries and zero preemptions. There was no missing traffic. There was a broken meter.

The reconciliation had been comparing against the coding agent's own report of its own usage — and an agent under-counts its own hidden requests. It discards summaries it asked for and was billed for. It retries first turns and drops them. Its delegated sub-agents report zero-on-zero on every record. The distortion concentrates in 3 of 32 instances, which is why it fails a sixteen-task run deterministically and lets a four-task run pass by luck — roughly 70% of the time.

Why it went unnoticed is the part worth stealing. Our own per-request metrics file was 0 bytes in every run ever recorded, because its writer only fires on an API route this traffic never uses. The reconciliation was written to fall back to the agent's self-report whenever our own numbers were missing — and they were always missing. A fallback that fires 100% of the time is not a fallback; it is the implementation. An empty file and a meter measuring nothing look identical from downstream.

The fix meters at the one hop that terminates every completion and therefore sees the engine's own count, and the numbers join a tamper-evident hash chain. The rule that came out of it: the fields are optional keys, not optional values. A ledger from before the change carries them on no row; one written now carries them on every row. A new-format ledger that metered nothing raises an error rather than falling back — because a meter recording nothing must fail its own audit.

What a sixteen-task quality gate can actually see

We gate behavioural quality on a fixed set of 16 real software-engineering tasks: does the agent still solve about as many as before? The gate passed this round — 9 of 16, in band, zero abandoned tasks. What makes it worth publishing is the reference distribution it was scored against.

Across 21 banked runs, resolve counts ran 7 to 10, median 9, standard deviation 0.91. Two of the 21 sit below the policy floor of 8 — so missing the floor by one is inside normal spread and should be read as "replicate this," not as proof of damage.

The sharper finding is what happens when you classify the tasks by how often they get solved:

That is the honest resolution of the instrument. A sixteen-task band can tell you the agent still works. It cannot tell you what a change cost you, and it should never be reported as if it can.

Close-Out

Where this started: 1.945× the floor, with a stated goal of cutting 40.9% more. The series ends without hitting that goal, and with a better understanding of why than hitting it would have produced.

What the series leaves behind

Not the numbers. These are the rules it paid for, and they cost real time each:

Measurement

Test at the width you deploy at. A per-user lever is invisible at one user by construction, and we nearly filed a 7% speedup as a null because of it.

Design

Scope a candidate narrowly and the rest of the run becomes a control group. Free placebo, same machine, same session, no extra runs to schedule.

Instruments

A meter recording nothing must fail its own audit, not fall back quietly. And an explicit zero must override a sidecar, or a dead instrument reads as a clean result.

Process

Audit your own list before you climb it. Three of our four largest planned optimisations were not there, and all three failed on a defect in the derivation rather than in the hardware.

The last one is the cheapest and it is the one we learned latest. Re-deriving three published numbers took a fraction of the time that building against them would have. If a lever is worth a week of engineering, it is worth an afternoon of checking that it exists.

Threats to Validity

An asymmetry worth naming: the refutations in this volume are better-evidenced than the claims. Killing a number needs one demonstrated defect; a replacement number carries the usual uncertainty.

The headline speedup

Sealed, but the paired reading is one draw

The −27.03 ms is sealed across four paired passes and is the number to quote. The −29.50 ms from the timing pair is a single paired difference, and the width-3 control shows 7.71 ms of arm-to-arm wobble on its own. The placebo constrains where the effect is, not how precisely it is known.

The corrected floor

Marked not-citable by its own author

The floor artifact carries NOT CITABLE and gpu_touched=false. It changes no measured wall — only the denominator. And a floor is a lower bound on time, not a reachable target: it assumes every byte moves once at full speed with zero latency, launch cost or imperfect overlap. Nothing reaches it.

Conversation length

Imported, not re-measured

The per-user context length is taken from the single-request measurement, because the four-request capture actively rejects the inversion that would re-measure it — that rejection is the load-imbalance finding. The sensitivity table bounds it from 12k to a 44.3k hard ceiling and every entry moves the conclusion the same way, but the central value is borrowed.

Profiled numbers

Upper bounds

Component costs at four requests are inflated by the profiler by 18.28 ms/step in total. The unnamed-family band carries that correction explicitly; the per-component table does not, and those are upper bounds. No profiled figure may be compared as a regression against an unprofiled one.

Reducible estimates

Judgement, published as judgement

The 33–62 ms in the unnamed family is per-class engineering estimate, not measurement, and nothing has been built against any of it. It is broken out per class so a reader can reject one class without discarding the rest.

Load imbalance

Inferred from gaps, not counters

The bandwidth rejection comes from regressing across twelve isolated ingestion gaps, not from hardware memory counters. The interval's upper edge is +0.32 ns against a 15.01 ns floor, which is a wide margin, but a counter run remains the decisive test and has not been done.

Quality at n=16

The band is coarse and now quantified

Eleven of sixteen tasks are constants across 21 runs. The gate detects a broken agent; it cannot detect one or two lost solves. Every behavioural claim in this series inherits that, including the earlier claims that a quality regression had closed — those survive, but with a wider error bar than they were originally stated with.

Scope

One model, one machine

Everything here is a 27-billion-parameter FP8 hybrid model on a 48-processor unified-memory machine at 273 GB/s. The method generalises. The wave arithmetic, the boundary between shared and per-user cost, and every millisecond do not.
