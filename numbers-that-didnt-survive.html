The Numbers That Didn't Survive

Auditing Our Own Ladder at Batch Four — Three Refuted Levers, a Hardware Floor Wrong in Three Directions at Once, and the Placebo Design That Reversed a Null

Vol. IX published a ranked ladder of everything left to optimise in a speculative decode step, priced against the machine's own memory floor, and concluded that the sum of it could not close the gap. This volume is what happened when we built the top of that ladder. All three of its largest legal items returned null — not "smaller than hoped," but refuted, each by a specific defect in how the original number was derived. Then the work moved to four concurrent requests, where almost every conclusion changed shape: the floor those ratios were divided by was wrong in three separate directions, an entire kernel family larger than attention had never been named, the biggest attention lever was ranked on a premise that turned out to be false, and a result that measured null at one batch width reversed at another. The series closes here, at an honest 1.83× and 2.57× the floor, with two levers banked and every refutation on the record.

TL;DR: The ladder audited. Item 1, the 10.08 ms host tail — a mean-versus-median artifact. Fifteen steps out of 1146 had a chunked-prefill forward land inside the measurement window; the median tail is 3.59 ms, and what is in it is a precondition for the next forward, not deferrable bookkeeping, so the lever's mechanism dies outright. Item 2, the 3.45 ms scan fusion — the deployed two-launch schedule is already critical-path-optimal (dependency depth 12, makespan 5 + 7 = 12), and the model that priced it divided real time by phantom work: its 82 padded slots never execute. Item 3, the conv plumbing — costed against a number that predated its own fix; the real figure is 0.542 ms/step, 0.2% of the envelope. One lever did survive, found where nobody was looking: the scan is bandwidth-bound on an fp32 state handoff worth 56.1% of the kernel, and removing it is −8.98 ms/step at width four. The denominator. "B4 sits at 3.0× the floor, B1 at 1.94×" was wrong three ways: B4 was divided by a floor for weights it never reads (which makes it look worse, 3.19×), and a weights-only floor is structurally unfair to a wide batch because weights are batch-invariant while every other mandatory byte is per-request. Honest: 1.833× and 2.574×, a gap of 1.40× not 1.55× — and per request, B4 is already 1.81× closer to the floor than B1 is. Where width costs. Not the GEMM: it is batch-invariant and grows 6% from one request to four. It is attention (3.26×) and the recurrent scan (3.32×). And attention is not KV-bandwidth-bound at width four — the marginal KV token costs ≤0.32 ns against a 15.0 ns DRAM floor, 47× cheaper than physics allows. Its real cost is load imbalance: a wave holds all four requests and finishes when the longest one does. The placebo. A candidate that measured null at four tasks came back −29.50 ms/step at width four, and because it is served at exactly one operating point, every narrower step in the same window is a natural control — both of which ran against it, so the raw delta understates.

Abstract

This is a volume about being wrong in public, and about the specific mechanisms that made us wrong. Vol. IX ended with a ranked ladder: thirteen mechanisms priced against a 237 ms decode step, each labelled legal, blocked, or dead, summing to a 19.4 ms/step ceiling that could not close the gap to the campaign's target. The three largest legal items on it were worth 16.5 ms between them. We built all three. All three returned null, and none of them failed for the reason a lever usually fails.

The host tail — the ladder's largest item at 10.08 ms of GPU idle — was a mean over a bimodal population. The profiling markers wrap only the speculative-decode path, so on the 1.3% of steps where a chunked-prefill forward happened to land between one decode step's drafter and the next one's verify, that entire forward was counted as tail. Fifteen steps out of 1146, one of them 3.06 seconds long, carried a mean of 11.98 ms over a median of 3.59. Worse for the lever: re-reading what is actually inside the window shows it is the next step's input preparation and attention-metadata build — a precondition for the following forward, not deferrable bookkeeping. The prescribed mechanism, "move it off the critical path," has nothing it can move. The same re-derivation caught a second error of our own making: 1.945 ms/step of idle attributed to graph launch is a profiler artifact. Measured directly on the same machine, launching the 1894-node verify graph costs 3.15 microseconds. The capture's figure was 617× the truth.

The scan fusion — 3.45 ms — failed differently and more instructively. The deployed two-launch schedule is already critical-path-optimal: the tree's dependency depth is 12 and the makespan is 5 + 7 = 12, because the deepest second-level path hangs off the last first-level export node, so the barrier between levels delays nothing. And the cost model that produced 3.45 had normalised by padded slots that never execute — the kernel's trip count is a per-path device-loaded length, not the padded maximum, so the model was dividing real time by phantom work. Forcing the phantom work to be real costs 401.89 µs against 158.30 deployed. But the same probe found a lever that was never on the ladder: the scan is bandwidth-bound on an fp32 state handoff worth 56.1% of the kernel's cost, and a parked, never-timed single-launch variant removes it — byte-identical across four seeds and five input regimes, worth −1.92 ms/step at batch one and −8.98 ms/step at width four.

Then the subject changed. Almost everything above is batch-one work, and the deployment regime is four concurrent requests. At width four the conclusions do not merely scale — several of them invert. The floor the ratios were divided by was wrong in three separate directions at once: the wide arms were being divided by a weight scenario for a drafter head they do not launch (which makes them look worse, not better), and a weights-only floor is structurally unfair to a wide batch, because weight traffic is batch-invariant while every other mandatory byte — attention KV, recurrent state carry, logits, residual stream — is per-request and therefore four times larger. Building the floor honestly, by the same rule the batch-one roofline used, gives 1.833× and 2.574×, a gap of 1.40× rather than the published 1.55×. Per request, the wide operating point is already 1.81× closer to the floor than the narrow one.

Three more findings came out of the width-four capture, and each retires an assumption. The cost of concurrency is not the GEMM — Vol. IX's central negative result survives and strengthens, because the GEMM re-reads the same weights at any width and grows only 6% from one request to four. The cost is attention (3.26×) and the recurrent scan (3.32×). An entire kernel family, 83.9 ms/step and larger than attention, had never been named — 184 distinct kernels firing 4,777 times per step, of which 9.2 ms is sampler math running on a padded fixed-width buffer regardless of how many requests are resident, and which later turned out to be our own tree-accept walk rather than the framework's. And attention is not KV-bandwidth-bound at width four: the marginal KV token costs at most 0.32 ns of attention time against a 15.0 ns DRAM floor, 47× cheaper than physics permits if it were reading that KV from memory. Attention's real cost is cross-request load imbalance — a wave holds all four requests and finishes when the longest finishes — which retires the byte-traffic premise the leading attention lever had been ranked on.

The volume closes with three pieces of measurement hygiene that are the most portable things in it: a per-width placebo that turns a single-arm speed claim into a difference-in-differences design and reversed a null; an instrument that had been recording nothing for months while a downstream reconciliation passed on it; and a quality-control pass over 21 banked arms showing that of a 16-task evidence set, eleven tasks are constants — seven always resolve and four never do — so the band's real discriminating power rests on five.

How every refutation here is reported: the original claim and where it was published; the defect in how the number was derived, named precisely; the corrected quantity; and what, if anything, survives. Every refutation first reproduces the original reduction exactly before disagreeing with it — the host-tail re-derivation reproduces Vol. IX's step, verify, committer, drafter and boundary figures to the millisecond, so the disagreement is demonstrably about framing rather than arithmetic. A refutation that cannot reproduce what it refutes is a different measurement, not a correction.

Contribution

A published post-mortem on a published ladder. Three refuted levers with the derivation defect named in each case, a hardware floor rebuilt honestly and shown to have been wrong in three directions simultaneously, an unnamed kernel family opened, a false premise retired from underneath the leading attention lever, and a placebo design that converts a single-arm speed number into something with a control.

This page is the sequel to Vol. IX and closes the series. It exists because the alternative — quietly not mentioning that the ladder we published did not survive being built — would make the previous volume worse rather than better. The refutation of a number is a result, and it belongs at the same address as the number.

The Ladder, Audited

Ground rule: each refutation reproduces the original reduction exactly before disagreeing with it. The host-tail re-derivation reproduces Vol. IX's step (237.248), verify (155.829), committer (20.705), drafter (35.131) and boundary (1.224) figures to the millisecond off the same 218,692,330-byte capture. The disagreement is the framing, not the arithmetic.

Vol. IX ranked thirteen mechanisms. Its top three legal items were worth 16.5 of the 19.4 ms/step ceiling. This is what each one turned out to be.

Item 1 — the host tail: a mean over a bimodal population

The ladder called this "the single largest addressable block in the trace": 11.969 ms of boundary, 10.080 ms of GPU idle, ~7.8 ms of pure Python outside any CUDA call.

The tail is 3.46 ms, not 10.08. The median of the GPU-projected tail is 3.588 ms; the 11.977 ms mean is carried by 15 steps out of 1146 — 1.3% — whose "tail" is not a tail at all but a chunked-prefill forward, up to 3.06 seconds long with 3608 eager launches. The cause is a marker-scope bug rather than a scheduling one: the profiling markers wrap only the speculative-decode path, so a prefill forward lands between one decode step's drafter and the next decode step's verify, and the reduction attributes all of it to the boundary. On decode-cadence steps the honest figures are 3.667 ms of host wall, 3.458 ms of GPU idle, and 2.857 ms of host CPU inside no CUDA call.

The mechanism dies for a second and independent reason. Reading what is actually inside the window — 154 CUDA operations buying 0.209 ms of GPU work, plus around 45 Python blocks and one stream synchronisation — identifies it as step N+1's state update, input preparation, and attention-metadata build. That is a precondition for the next forward, not deferrable bookkeeping. Vol. IX's prescription was "move it off the critical path"; there is nothing in it that can move. Only its cost is attackable, and its cost is a third of what was published.

And a second error, ours The graph-launch idle that wasn't there
Published

1.945 ms/step of GPU idle attributed to launching the 1894-node verify graph — a figure that also implicitly priced every future graph-node-count reduction.

Measured directly

Launching that graph on the same GB10 costs 3.15 microseconds — 0.443 ns per node plus 2.31 µs fixed, maximum residual 0.04 µs. Interleaving a second graph adds 0.16 µs, so there is no re-upload effect either.

The ratio

617×. The capture's figure was a CUPTI artifact, and Vol. IX carried it into a threats-to-validity box that said profiler inflation "should not be quoted" without noticing it was already inside a headline.

What it prices

Graph node count is not a host lever at all. The parked verify-side fusion that removes 192 nodes per step is worth 0.085 microseconds of host time.

Item 2 — the scan fusion: a cost model built on work that never runs

The ladder priced fusing the two-level recurrent scan into a single ready-flag launch at 3.45 ms/step, and was emphatic about the design detail: per-node ready flags rather than a grid barrier, because "that distinction is the 3.45 ms."

The distinction is worth zero, because the deployed schedule is already critical-path-optimal. The tree's dependency depth is 12. The deployed makespan is the length of level 0 plus the longest level-1 path: 5 + 7 = 12. The barrier between the levels delays nothing, because the deepest level-1 path hangs off node 14 — the last level-0 export node. Waves, executed node-steps, thread-block-steps and handoff bytes are all invariant under the fusion. Ready flags let a consumer start earlier; they do not remove a producer's store or a consumer's load between different thread blocks.

Why the 3.45 looked real is the more useful half. The model normalised by "82 padded slots" — and those slots never execute. The path kernel's trip count is a per-path length loaded from the device, not the padded maximum. So the model divided real time by phantom work, producing a 2.944 ns/unit rate that was never achievable; the true rate is 7.63 ns per thread-block-step. This was tested rather than argued: forcing all eleven level-1 paths to genuinely run seven slots costs 401.89 µs against 158.30 µs deployed.

Figure 1Why the barrier costs nothing: the deepest second-level path hangs off the last first-level node

Figure 1 is why the lever was worth nothing. Ready flags help a consumer start before the whole producer level finishes — but the schedule's critical path runs through the last node of level 0 and then through the longest level-1 path, so the barrier sits at a point where the critical path was going to be anyway. The short level-1 paths (dashed) do gain slack, and they already had it. The published 3.45 ms came from a cost model that measured this schedule against an idealised one that could not be faster.

Item 3 — the conv plumbing: a brief costed against a pre-fix number

The rung brief costed roughly 340 per-step launches in the convolution commit path. Against the merged stack, the convolution commit is one launch per event — corroborated at 1.00 instances per step in the banked attribution — and the whole thing is 0.542 ms/step, 0.2% of the envelope. The 48-layer Python committer the brief named is not on the served path at all, and a wiring test already pinned that. The lever was unsatisfiable by construction.

The 340 launches are real but live elsewhere: 528.3 launches per step of per-layer verify plumbing. Those are graph nodes, where the measured floor is 213 ns, so collapsing all of them recovers at most 0.112 ms/step. What shipped instead is a batched slot publish worth 0.016 ms/step at width four — and the commit message is worth quoting for its honesty: it is "400× below the measured B4 noise floor and is documented as such — it ships because it is free and provable, not because it moves a gate."

The one lever this audit found

The same probe that killed the fusion found something the ladder never listed. The scan is bandwidth-bound on its fp32 state handoff: export writes of 46.0 µs plus parent reads of 82.3 µs out of 228.74 µs per layer — 56.1% of the two-launch kernel's cost is moving state between the levels, not computing. A parked, never-timed single-launch variant removes the handoff entirely and measures 188.64 µs per layer.

The three derivation defects, stated generally Each is easy to reproduce in any performance study
Defect 1

A mean over a bimodal population

1.3% of samples were a different kind of event, and they carried a 3.3× inflation of the mean. Report a median alongside any per-step mean, and check what the extreme tail of the distribution actually is before optimising against its average.

Defect 2

Normalising by work that never executes

Padded capacity is not executed work. A rate computed as real-time-over-padded-units is not a rate, and any model built on it will over-promise by exactly the padding factor.

Defect 3

Costing against a superseded baseline

The number in the brief was true when written and false when acted on. A lever brief should carry the commit its measurement was taken at, and be re-derived against the merged stack before anyone builds.

The Denominator Was Wrong in Three Directions

Order matters here: the correction that makes the wide batch look worse is applied first. A paper that only applies the flattering correction is not doing an audit.

Status: the source artifact marks itself NOT CITABLE, gpu_touched=false. It changes no measured wall — only the number those walls are divided by.

The published comparison was: batch one sits at 1.94× its hardware floor, width four sits at 3.0×, so the wide operating point has much more room. Three separate things are wrong with that, and they do not all point the same way.

Correction 1 — the wrong floor scenario (makes width four look worse)

The floor ledger publishes several weight-only scenarios. Batch one divides by 119.658 ms — the scenario with five 64K-subset draft heads. The width-four artifacts divide by 126.514 ms, the scenario with one full-vocabulary root head. But the sealed wide arms launch with the root drafter head set to the 64K subset; the launcher's own metadata records draft_vocab_root=1, draft_vocab_k=65536 and mandatory_weight_floor_ms=119.658015414. The wide arms were being divided by a floor for 1.87 GB of weights they never read.

On the same basis, width four is at 3.19×, not 3.01. Fixing the basis makes it look 6% worse — and that correction has to be taken before the one that makes it look better, or the second is not credible.

Correction 2 — a weights-only floor is structurally unfair to a wide batch

The ledger says so about itself: non-weight costs are excluded. And weight traffic is batch-invariant — the same 32.667 GB moves whether the step serves one request or four. That is confirmed by the capture rather than assumed: the FP8 GEMM grows 1.08× from width one to width four and the output-head GEMM is flat above width two. Meanwhile every other mandatory byte is per-request, and therefore four times larger at width four: attention KV re-reads, drafter KV re-reads, KV writes, the recurrent state carry, logits materialisation, the residual stream round trip.

A denominator built only from the invariant term flatters the narrow batch and penalises the wide one by construction. So the honest floor applies the same rule the attention roofline used — mandatory unique bytes over 273 GB/s — to every term, with the same per-request context for both. The geometry is asserted against the served config and the tool raises rather than emitting if anything mismatches; the check that matters is that the same geometry re-derives the published attention floor to 4.3285 ms against the ledger's 4.33.

Vol. IX's central negative result survives, and gets stronger. The GEMM is untouchable at batch one because it is already at 85.8% of the memory roofline with no scheduler lever against it — and at width four it is also, and separately, not the thing concurrency costs. The Kernel Family Nobody Had Named

Why it stayed invisible: every reduction in this series ranked kernels by name. A family of 184 distinct small kernels, none individually large, sums to more than attention and appears in no ranking that sorts by single-kernel cost.

The gap analysis had been leaving 17.9% of the window in a residual family called "other" and not opening it. Opened, it is 184 distinct kernels, roughly 3.06 million instances, 83.92 ms/step at width four — 19.5% of the profiled step, the second-largest item after the GEMM, and larger than attention.

Because the bucket is where three million tiny launches live, it is also where per-launch profiler cost concentrates, so it is bounded rather than point-quoted: total width-four inflation is 18.28 ms/step, putting the bucket at 65.6–83.9 ms/step, central 80.2.

Two findings inside it, and the second one has a twist.

(a) The sampler runs on a padded buffer, whatever the width

One kernel — torch's generic cumulative sum — costs 6.93 ms/step at width two and 6.93 ms/step at width four. Bit-identical width-invariance, and it does not appear at width one at all. The generic softmax alongside it goes 2.27 → 2.29. Together, 9.2 ms/step of sampler math is being done on a fixed four-by-thirty-two-row buffer regardless of how many requests are actually resident. At width three — 44% of captured steps — a quarter of it is provably wasted. And it sits beside the framework's already-fused top-k/top-p kernel, which costs 4.66 ms for the same population: a generic route running in parallel with the fused route that exists to replace it.

That was the reading, and the recommended action was to delete the generic path. Then the follow-up work went looking for it and found something better and more embarrassing: the 9.2 ms is not the framework's sampler at all. It is ours. It is the tree-accept walk — our own committer-side softmax and cumulative sum over the full vocabulary, 24 of them per step, running on 4 of 48 SMs. The bucket was mislabelled because the kernels are torch generics and the natural assumption was that torch had launched them.

(b) 4,777 launches per step

One 5.5-microsecond elementwise kernel fires 768 times per step for 4.21 ms. Add the gathers (226), direct copies (278), batched concatenations (192) and index selects (196), and the bucket launches 4,777 kernels per step at width four. This is structurally the same problem the single-launch scan fold was built to solve, and the scan gives the measured precedent: 41.35 → 32.37 ms for a launch-count consolidation. Consolidation is worth roughly what it looks like it is worth.

The lesson

A residual bucket is not a rounding error. Rank kernels by family as well as by name, or a family of small kernels larger than your second-biggest named item will sit in the residual for a year.

Attention Is Not Bandwidth-Bound at Width Four

What this retires: the leading attention lever was ranked on a byte-traffic argument. The premise of that argument is tested here directly, and rejected.

Both this series' attention floors — the batch-one roofline and the width-four extrapolation — rest on an antecedent that was never tested: if KV traffic scales with batch, then the floor is such-and-such. The width-four capture can test the antecedent, because a chunked-prefill gap admits a known number of KV tokens and the steps on either side are clean.

The marginal KV token costs at most 0.32 ns of attention time against a 15.0 ns DRAM floor — 47× cheaper than physics allows if the kernel were reading that KV from memory. Proportionality between attention time and KV bytes is rejected at width four.

The grid semantics behind that are proven rather than assumed: there is no split-combine kernel anywhere in the trace, so the split count is one and the grid is (row blocks, batch, heads); and the batch-dimension population is exactly 3,616 = 226 × 16 launches against 225 width-four steps × 16 attention layers.

The mechanism

Attention uses 102,400 bytes of shared memory — the opt-in maximum — so one thread block per SM, and a wave is 48 blocks. At width four the launch is 96 blocks: exactly two waves, and each wave contains all four requests. A wave finishes when its longest request finishes.

attention cost ≈   max(context)   and cross-request imbalance

not   Σ context

So the ~63 ms of attention headroom is real, but it is not a bandwidth-efficiency problem and it will not yield to a layout change. It is a scheduling problem: request-balanced work assignment, or split-KV at width four — a lever that has never been on any ladder in this series. And it means the leading layout-based candidate should be expected to move less than a byte-traffic argument predicts.

Which makes the next section more interesting, not less: that candidate was re-tested at width four anyway, and it moved a great deal.

The Per-Width Placebo

The problem this solves: a paired arm-versus-arm speed delta on an agentic workload is one draw, contaminated by acceptance drift and co-residency luck. Vol. IX's promotion criterion caught one of those. This design catches the rest.

A query-head pairing candidate for attention had measured null on the four-task screen on 2026-08-11. Re-tested at the width-four operating point it is faster by 29.50 ms/step — 411.26 → 381.75, −7.17% — against a sealed four-pass minimum detectable effect of 4.20 ms and a pre-registered target of 6.97. Per-request throughput +6.04%, aggregate +5.35%.

That is exactly the kind of reversal that should not be believed on its own. Here is the design that makes it credible.

The candidate serves at exactly one width

The candidate's scope token restricts it to the full-graph width-four dispatch. Every narrower step inside the same window runs the identical stock dispatch on both arms. So the narrow widths are a natural placebo — drawn from the same arms, the same hosts, the same window, the same wall chain, with no separate control run to schedule and no cross-session comparison to defend.

Two instruments agreeing, and one caveat that stands Why this is believable, and how far
Independent corroboration

The stock arm's own width 3→4 cost is +49.17 ms. The Nsight attribution independently put attention's width-four cost at +48.4 ms — a different instrument on a different basis, agreeing to 1.6%.

Physically bounded

The candidate removes 76% of the width-scaling cost, and the 37 ms sits inside the ~52 ms of structural headroom the attribution measured above attention's floor. The result is bounded rather than impossible.

Both old confounds absent

The 2026-08-11 null decomposed into +77.3% acceptance drift and 26.5% lower co-residency. Here acceptance is flat (+1.16%) and events per step are matched (3.633 vs 3.610, −0.65%) — neither mechanism can generate this delta.

Stated honestly

n=1 paired draw. The MDE is a four-pass between-pass quantity, not the uncertainty of a single paired difference — and the width-three control shows 7.71 ms of arm-to-arm variation all by itself.

The reducer now computes the placebo automatically and writes its verdict into the payload: if any untreated width gains at or above the MDE, the verdict says so loudly rather than leaving a reader to notice. A control width needs at least 100 steps to drive the correction, so a one-step width cannot move it. This was also where a review caught the design's own footgun — an earlier revision let a treated width serve as its own placebo control, which would have laundered the effect into its own baseline.

Sealed separately and independently, the padded candidate is worth +27.03 ms/step (standard deviation 13.77, one-sided 95% lower bound +10.82), dual-gated at three successive commits, and is now the width-four production default.

The Meter That Had Been Recording Nothing

Companion to Vol. IX's trap flags: that volume documented a deleted lever whose wiring survived, so setting it was accepted and silently ignored. This is the same class, one layer up — an instrument whose output file was empty in every arm ever recorded.

The campaign's token reconciliation closed with no slack against the agent's self-reported usage, and had done so for months. On the width-four screen it stopped closing: a gap of 189,780 prompt tokens and 5,654 generation tokens appeared and was provisionally filed as unattributed traffic — the sort of thing that suggests requests leaking outside the ledger.

Request-level diagnosis proved the opposite: 639 of 639 engine requests were ledger-attributed, with zero retries and zero preemptions. There was no unattributed traffic. There was a bad meter.

The reconciliation had been comparing against a third-party agent's own accounting of itself, and that accounting under-credits its own hidden requests in at least three ways — a context compaction the engine served and billed whose summary the agent then discarded, retried-and-discarded first turns, and delegated sub-agent turns that self-report zero-on-zero on every record. One task alone carried an uncredited 1,550-token request against a 712-token hidden credit. The distortion is concentrated in 3 of 32 instances, which is why it fails a sixteen-task arm deterministically and lets a four-task gate pass by luck — the gates had been reconciling at probability roughly 0.7, not by structure.

Why nobody noticed for months The failure mode worth stealing
The dead file

A per-request metrics file was 0 bytes in every arm ever recorded, because its writer only runs on an API route this traffic never takes. A file that is always empty and a meter that measured nothing look identical from downstream.

Why it passed

The reconciliation fell back to the agent's self-report whenever the engine fields were absent — and they were always absent. A fallback that fires 100% of the time is not a fallback; it is the implementation.

The fix

Meter at the hop that terminates every completion — the proxy sees the engine's own count. The counts join the tamper-evident hash chain, so editing one invalidates the chain from that row on.

The doctrine

The fields are optional keys, not optional values. A ledger predating them carries them on no row; one written now carries them on every row. A new-schema ledger that metered nothing raises rather than falling back — because a meter recording nothing must fail its own audit. That indistinguishability is precisely what hid the dead meter.

This is the second instrument-shaped failure this series has published and the more dangerous one, because the trap flag of Vol. IX would at least have produced a wrong measurement. This produced a passing audit. Twenty-one tests now pin it, nineteen of which fail at the parent commit; the two that do not are the pair asserting the old meter is broken.

What a Sixteen-Task Band Can Actually See

Extends Vol. IX's Tier-B limit: that volume said the behavioural band "detects a broken agent, not a costly one," citing a Wilson interval. This quantifies it against 21 banked arms, and the answer is worse than the interval implies.

The quality-control pass over the width-four levers passed all three gates — 9 of 16 resolved, in band; no garble regression; zero give-ups. That is the honest resolution of the instrument, and it is the strongest available argument for the two gates Vol. IX insisted on keeping alongside it: a logit-deviation bound, which has tight resolution, and acceptance parity, which is the only measurement that reads a perturbation through the sampler. A behavioural band built on sixteen tasks of which eleven are constants can tell you that the agent still works. It cannot tell you what a change cost.

It also puts a useful floor under a claim this series has made repeatedly. When Vol. IX reported "zero give-ups across four arms," the reference distribution now says four give-ups across twenty-one arms is the background rate — so zero-across-four is consistent with background rather than being strong evidence on its own. The give-up regression of Volumes V and VI was five give-ups in a single arm, which remains far outside this distribution; the claim that it closed survives, but it survives with a wider error bar than it was originally stated with.

Five open items, documented rather than closed

  1. A production re-gate at current head is required before the next production serve. The doctrine bank

What a campaign leaves behind is not usually its numbers. These are the rules this series paid for:

Provenance

Head-bound credentials — every commit forces a re-gate, so batch merges into trains. Manifest binding is settledness, not the working tree's status.

Failure

Refuse at the right layer: qualification refuses, serving degrades. A fail-closed check placed one layer too low took down an engine this series. Boot-refusal probes mirror the runner, not the arm.

Measurement

Workload-matching, not same-pool — paired passes absorb trajectory drift. Meter on your own instrument, never a third party's self-report.

Instruments

Authoritative zero: an explicit zero must veto a sidecar. A gate that can only see the bit-stable operation is blind by construction — widen it before you trust it.

And the one this volume adds: audit your own ladder before you climb it. Three of the three largest items we published did not survive being built, and every one of them failed on a defect in the derivation rather than in the hardware. The cost of finding that out was small; the cost of not finding out would have been the entire next campaign.

Threats to Validity

Note the asymmetry: the refutations in this volume are better-evidenced than the claims. Killing a number needs only one demonstrated defect; the replacement numbers carry the usual uncertainty.

The honest floor

Not citable, by its own authors

The floor artifact marks itself NOT CITABLE, gpu_touched=false. It changes no measured wall — only the denominator. And the floor is a lower bound on time, not an achievable target: it assumes every mandatory byte moves once at full bandwidth with zero latency, launch cost, recompute or imperfect overlap. Nothing reaches it.

Context length

Imported, not re-measured

The per-request context is taken from the batch-one ladder because the width-four capture actively rejects the inversion that would re-measure it — that rejection is the load-imbalance finding. The sensitivity table bounds it across 12k to a 44.3k hard cap, and every entry moves the conclusion the same way, but the central value is borrowed.

The width-four result

One paired draw

−29.50 ms is a single paired difference. The MDE it clears is a four-pass between-pass quantity, which is not the uncertainty of one draw — and the width-three control shows 7.71 ms of arm-to-arm variation on its own. The placebo constrains where the effect is, not how precisely it is known.

Profiled numbers

Upper bounds, with the correction stated

Width-four component costs are CUPTI-profiled and inflated by 18.28 ms/step in total. The other-bucket band carries that correction explicitly; other per-component figures do not, and are upper bounds. No profiled number may be compared as a regression against an unprofiled wall.

Reducibility bands

Judgement, published as judgement

The 33–62 ms in the other bucket is per-class engineering estimate, not measurement, and no candidate has been built against any of it. It is published per class so a reader can reject one class without discarding the rest.

Load imbalance

Inferred from gaps, not counters

The KV-bandwidth rejection comes from regressing across twelve isolated prefill gaps, not from DRAM or L2 counters. The confidence interval's upper edge is +0.32 ns, comfortably under the 15.0 ns floor, but a counter run remains the decisive test and has not been done.

The refutations themselves

Two are analysis, one is measured

The scan-fusion refutation is measured — the phantom-work claim was tested by forcing the phantom work to be real. The host-tail refutation is a re-reduction of the same capture plus a direct graph-launch measurement. The conv-plumbing refutation is a source and census reading. None required a new campaign, which is a strength for cost and a weakness for independence.

Series scope

One model, one machine

Everything here is a 27B FP8 GDN hybrid on a 48-SM unified-memory GB10 at 273 GB/s. The method generalises; the wave arithmetic, the batch-invariance boundary and every millisecond do not.
