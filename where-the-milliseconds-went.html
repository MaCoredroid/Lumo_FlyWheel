Volume IX / illustrated systems paper

Where Every Millisecond Went

A Full Accounting of a 232-Millisecond Decode Step — What the Campaign Bought, Where the Remaining Time Sits, and Why the Largest Consumer of It Has No Legal Lever Left

Vol. VIII inventoried sixteen speed attempts and ended with a deficit that had changed shape: no longer a per-event tax, but a fixed per-step one. This volume is what happened when we went after that fixed cost with a real profiler and a hard target — the mandatory weight-read floor of the machine itself. The result is the most complete accounting this series has produced: every phase of the step priced, every remaining lever ranked legal, blocked, or dead, and a byte model cross-checked five independent ways against 4.3 million profiled kernel rows. The campaign's own headline is a win — the step is down roughly a third and the quality regression of earlier volumes is gone — and the accounting's headline is a refusal: the single kernel class that consumes 48% of the step has no legal optimization remaining, and everything still legal adds up to a fifth of what closing the target would require.

TL;DR: What the campaign bought. The batch-one step is 232.780 ms at 24.72 tok/s, accepting 4.754 drafts per event and committing 5.754 tokens — down from roughly 342 ms/step eleven days earlier on the prior arm, with the drafter cut 61%, the verify forward 27%, and the committer 25%. Quality, which earlier volumes lost, is back: four arms on one binary at 9–11 of 16 resolved with zero give-ups and zero crashes, against a garble-era low of 6/16 with five give-ups. Deployment concurrency was unblocked too — effective decode batch 1.3 → 3.20, byte-identical at batch one. What the accounting found. Against a mandatory weight-read floor of 119.658 ms/step the step sits at 1.945×, and the profiler says the biggest piece of that is untouchable: the target GEMM is 114.812 ms/step, 48.4% of the envelope, already fully graph-captured, already at 85.8% of the 273 GB/s roofline, with no wave-quantization penalty at all — the shapes that leave a sixth of the machine idle for the kernel's whole duration are at the top of the bandwidth range, and achieved bandwidth is flat across a 6.8× spread in block count. Every scheduler lever held in reserve against it — persistent kernels, megakernel fusion, stream ordering, L2 policy — is worth a combined 0.009 ms/step. What is left. The recoverable time is elsewhere and mostly host-side: a 10.08 ms/step idle tail where the GPU waits on Python, 3.45 ms from fusing a two-level scan whose first level is 7.36× less efficient than its second, and an attention kernel running 4.93× above its own key-value floor because each KV byte is staged twelve times. Summed and priced honestly, the entire legal ladder is 19.4 ms/step against a 95.2 ms/step gap to the acceptance cap. It does not close. That conclusion, not a speedup, is this volume's deliverable — along with Tier B, a second kernel-admission tier for candidates that cannot be byte-identical by construction rather than by defect, which unblocks up to 17 ms of the wall and still does not change the arithmetic.

Abstract

This volume prices a speculative decode step. Not a lever, not an ablation — the whole step, phase by phase and kernel class by kernel class, against the one number the hardware will not negotiate: the time it takes to read the model's weights out of memory once. On a GB10 with 273 GB/s of unified LPDDR5X and 27 GB of FP8 weights, that mandatory read is 119.658 ms per step. Our step is 232.780 ms. Everything here is an account of the difference.

Two things are worth saying before the accounting, because they are what makes it possible. First, the campaign works. Over the weeks covered by Vol. VIII and this one, the batch-one step came down from roughly 342 ms to 232.8, the drafter fell by 61%, the deployment scheduler stopped serialising a four-request queue into an effective batch of 1.3, and the agentic quality regression that dominated Volumes V and VI closed completely — four arms on one binary now resolve 9–11 of 16 with zero give-ups, where the garble-era tree burned 9.6 agent-hours failing to converge on five tasks. Second, the accounting is only meaningful because of a rule that has governed every candidate in this series: exact math. A kernel is admitted when its output tensors are bit-for-bit identical to the incumbent's on a real authenticated event. That rule is why 232.780 and 119.658 can be compared at all, and it is also, as this volume shows, the binding constraint on everything left.

The accounting itself is built on a 219 MB Nsight capture — 4,324,314 GPU kernel rows, 4,105,181 CUDA runtime rows, 1,146 complete step instances — exported to SQL and reduced offline. Its central finding is a negative one, and it concerns the largest single consumer of time in the step. The target GEMM — the projections that make up the model's forward pass — is 114.812 ms/step, 48.4% of the envelope, and it has no legal scheduler lever. It is already one CUDA graph with a mean inter-node gap of 213 nanoseconds. It already runs at 85.8% of the memory roofline. And it has no wave-quantization penalty whatsoever: the launch shapes that leave eight of forty-eight streaming multiprocessors idle for the kernel's entire duration achieve 214.7–220.6 GB/s, at the top of a range whose other end — a shape running 5.67 waves — sits at 216.2. Achieved bandwidth is flat across a 6.8× spread in block count, so under-filling the GPU costs nothing here: the memory controller, not the SM count, is the limit. Persistent scheduling, megakernel fusion, graph-node fusion, stream ordering and L2 policy are worth a combined 0.009 ms/step against it.

The recoverable time is elsewhere, and it is mostly not on the GPU at all. A 10.08 ms/step idle tail sits after the drafter, of which roughly 7.8 ms is host Python outside any CUDA call. A two-level recurrent scan wastes 3.45 ms/step because its first level is 7.36× less efficient per unit of work than its second — a five-deep serial chain holding the whole GPU while doing 6% of the work. And the attention kernel runs 4.93× above its own key-value read floor, because forty-eight thread blocks stage the same four KV heads and each byte is therefore fetched twelve times. That last one is worth 17.03 ms and is blocked, not dead: the fix is a tile-geometry change that reassociates a floating-point reduction and therefore cannot be byte-identical.

Summed, ranked, and priced against the evidence, the full legal ladder is 19.4 ms/step — 10 to 14 realistic. The gap to the acceptance cap is 95.2 ms/step. Applying everything legal lands near 213 ms/step, still 1.55× the cap. The ladder cannot close the gap. Only arithmetic changes could, and the exact-math rule bars them. Publishing that conclusion, with the measurement that forces it, is the deliverable — alongside a composed six-kernel stack that measured null at 1.0016× stock step wall, six frontier branches disposed with zero merges, three kernel families deleted from the serving path, and Tier B: a second admission tier, with a hard mechanical invariant on the rejection sampler, for candidates whose only deviation from byte identity is the order in which floating-point numbers were added.

How every number in this paper is scoped: a measurement carries its arm, its batch size, and its basis in the same sentence, because in this workload all three move it. The profiled numbers come from an attribution capture where acceptance_valid=false — they localise time, they are not acceptance evidence, and host-side totals inside them carry profiler inflation that is called out where it matters. The step-wall numbers come from a separate canonical timing arm. Modelled figures are labelled modelled and carry the model that produced them. Where a bandwidth is quoted, it is derived from measured durations plus a byte model, not from hardware counters — the capture has no metric sampling — and that byte model is cross-checked five independent ways in the GEMM section.

Quality: the regression that closed

Volumes V and VI were substantially about a quality failure. A branched speculative tree that is lossless in the token sense was still degrading the agent — committing plausible-but-wrong neighbour identifiers inside otherwise-correct code, which the model would then notice and loop on. In its worst measured form the tree resolved 6 of 16 tasks with five give-ups, having burned 9.6 agent-hours grinding on tasks the linear baseline solved. Those give-ups were not quick bails: the shortest ran 39 minutes and the longest four and a half hours before emitting an empty patch.

Deployment: the concurrency that was being thrown away

A separate win, and an instructive one, because for weeks the campaign was measuring “batch four” runs that were not batch four. The scheduler's per-step token budget had been set exactly equal to the recurrent-state block size to satisfy a prefix-caching correctness constraint. Each step deducted the running decodes from that single budget first, leaving less than one block for the waiting queue — and the block-aligned split rounds a non-final prefill chunk down to a block multiple, so the remainder became zero and the waiting loop broke. Prefill and decode became mutually exclusive per step, and four queued agentic requests serialised into an effective decode batch of about 1.3.

The fix is two flags, and both are required: cap every request's per-step prefill chunk at one block, which enforces the correctness constraint directly and per-request, and only then raise the global budget. Raising the budget alone would have re-poisoned the cache. Measured effect: the running-request distribution moved from 80–85% at one to a mean of 3.20, and batch one stayed byte-identical because per-request chunk boundaries never moved.

Where the 15.8 milliseconds of idle actually sits

Idle time inside a step is the only thing a scheduling change can recover, so it is worth locating precisely. Breaking the step at its phase boundaries:

Three of the five boundaries are effectively zero. The residual is one tail after the drafter, plus a small bubble before it. Inside that tail the GPU does 1.889 ms of un-phased bookkeeping work — slot-mapping and block-zeroing kernels — and is idle for 10.080 ms. Host CUDA API time in the same window totals only 2.30 ms. Which means roughly 7.8 ms/step is host CPU outside any CUDA call at all: Python and framework work — sampler output handling, detokenisation, scheduling, request bookkeeping — while the GPU waits with an empty queue. It is the single largest addressable block in the trace, and it contains no arithmetic whatsoever.

The Target GEMM Has No Legal Lever

Why this section is long: it is the paper's central claim and it is negative. A negative result about the largest cost in a system deserves more evidence than a positive one, not less — so the byte model behind it is derived in the open and cross-checked five ways.

The projections inside the verify forward — the matrix multiplies that read the model's weights — run 256.2 instances per step and cost 114.812 ms/step. That is 48.4% of the entire step envelope. It is by a wide margin the largest single thing in the system, and every scheduler-level mechanism the campaign had been holding in reserve was aimed at it.

All of them are worth a combined 0.009 ms/step.

The four reasons, each independently measured

Already captured

There is no launch overhead to remove

All 293,549 target-GEMM instances in the capture are nodes inside one CUDA graph. The 256 GEMM instances per step carry a mean inter-node gap of 35.8 nanoseconds, totalling 9.17 microseconds per step. A persistent kernel or a megakernel cannot recover time that is not being spent.

No wave quantization

Under-filling the machine is free

The shapes launching 40 thread blocks leave 8 of 48 SMs idle for the whole kernel and achieve 214.7–220.6 GB/s. The shapes launching 272 achieve 216.2. Bandwidth is flat across a 6.8× range in block count and a 0.83–5.67 range in wave count. Perfect wave packing returns zero.

At the roofline

85.8% of what the memory can do

At the 5th percentile the GEMM already streams at 85.8% of the 273 GB/s ceiling; 82.4% at the mean. That is the practical limit for a streaming read on LPDDR5X, and the machine's memory is shared with the CPU.

Nothing to cache

L2 policy has no subject

24.83 GB is streamed per step with zero intra-step reuse. There is one stream, and the layer dependency chain admits no legal concurrent partner — the one latency-bound kernel that would pair well with it is its own consumer.

The second of those is the most load-bearing negative result in the document, and it is worth dwelling on because it inverts a standard intuition. Wave quantization is the well-known penalty for launching a thread-block count that does not divide evenly across the machine: the last wave runs with idle SMs, and you pay for a full wave's latency to do a partial wave's work. Here the worst-filled shapes are the fastest per byte. The reason is that in a weight-bound regime the tile that pads is the one holding the activations — at most a few hundred kilobytes — while the weight bytes streamed are unchanged. The kernel is limited by the memory controller, not by how many SMs are working, so leaving SMs idle costs nothing at all.

Where the Time Actually Is

The pattern: the three live items are a host tail, a load imbalance, and a redundant memory fetch. None of them is arithmetic, and none of them is in the kernel class that dominates the step.

If the largest cost is untouchable, the interesting question becomes which of the smaller ones are not. Three are worth their own treatment.

The host tail — 10.08 ms of an empty queue

The largest single addressable block in the trace is the idle after the drafter, and it is unusual in that it is almost entirely not a CUDA problem. Of the 11.969 ms boundary, 1.889 is un-phased GPU bookkeeping and 10.080 is idle. Host CUDA API time in the same window is 2.30 ms — 2.3 stream synchronisations at 1.685 ms, 92.8 kernel launches at 0.319 ms, 63.1 async copies at 0.219 ms. The remaining ~7.8 ms/step is host CPU outside any CUDA call: sampler output handling, detokenisation, the scheduler, request accounting.

What the stream synchronisations are waiting on is visible in the memory-copy census. Device-to-host traffic in the tail is 5.2 copies per step of 140 bytes each — the sampled-token readback. The host is stopping the pipeline to learn which tokens were accepted, in order to do bookkeeping that the next step's launch does not actually need. That is what makes the lever legal: nothing about it touches arithmetic, so the change must be byte-identical by construction and the existing byte gate is both sufficient and the acceptance criterion.

The design, and its falsification 10.08 ms ceiling, 5–7 realistic
Step 0

Instrument, do not guess. Four new markers inside the tail — sample readback, output processing, scheduling, KV bookkeeping — then re-run the existing offline reduction unchanged. This is the only step to take before choosing a design.

Step 1

Split submit from retire. The submit path enqueues the next step's verify graph as soon as draft tokens are on device; everything that only reads results moves to a retire queue on a second thread. Nothing in retire may gate a submit.

Step 2

Delete the sync from the submit path. Keep the accepted-token count on device and let the next step's slot-mapping and block-table kernels consume it directly, so the host never needs the value in order to enqueue.

Falsification

If GPU idle in the post-drafter window does not fall after step 1, the tail is a genuine data dependency rather than bookkeeping, and the lever dies. Re-run the same reduction and read the same boundary off the same table.

The scan — a first level that starves the machine

The recurrent scan runs in two launches per layer, 96 instances per step across the 48 Gated-DeltaNet layers, costing 12.357 ms/step. Level 0 scans the single heavy path rooted at the state carried in from the previous step; level 1 scans the eleven terminal paths that branch off the states level 0 exported. The obvious lever — fuse them into one launch — is worth nothing: the measured gap between the two launches is −0.128 µs. They are already back-to-back inside the verify graph.

The real finding is a load imbalance, and it only appears when the two levels are normalised by the work they actually do:

A grid-wide barrier between the levels re-serialises the schedule and returns only the ~0 µs launch gap. That distinction is the 3.45 ms.

Attention — a perfect wave with almost nothing in it, fetching every byte twelve times

The attention kernel costs 21.369 ms/step across 16 instances, one per attention layer. Its launch geometry is striking: a grid of exactly 48 blocks on 48 SMs — a perfect single wave — with a block size of 32 threads. One warp. Seventy-three kilobytes of dynamic shared memory pins it to one block per SM, so the machine runs with 32 resident threads per SM and 255 registers each. Launch overhead is nil: the mean gap to the preceding kernel is 101.9 ns, 1.6 µs across the whole step. Persistence across layers recovers 0.0016 ms. Dead as a launch lever.

The cost is elsewhere. Regressing duration against step index over all 1147 steps isolates the marginal cost of context growth at 0.4260 µs/step. With four KV heads, head-dimension 256, and bf16 storage — each pinned independently by evidence — a token costs 4096 bytes of KV per layer, and at 5.754 committed tokens per step the marginal traffic is 23,568 bytes. That works out to an achieved unique-byte throughput of 55.3 GB/s — 20.3% of the roofline.

Two levers exist and neither is simply available. An L2 persistence window on the KV region is legal but unquantified — pure launch policy, no arithmetic, and it attacks exactly the thing costing time; but this capture has no metric sampling, so the win cannot be modelled here and needs an L2 hit-rate run. Folding the two row blocks into one by doubling the row-block tile would halve the staging directly, and is blocked: it is a tile-geometry change and drifts bytes. The closest thing to a live candidate is a query-head pairing that cuts KV scans per group from six to three while claiming that total query rows, attention arithmetic, launched threads and launched warps per layer are all unchanged. If that invariance claim survives a byte gate it becomes legal and is worth a large fraction of the 17 ms. Its gate is pending. Until then it is blocked, not banked — and it is the reason Tier B exists.

The Null Result, and What We Deleted Because of It

The uncomfortable pairing: a six-kernel stack that all landed and all gated, measured together, moved the step wall by 0.16%. The ladder above explains why, and it was measured after.

Running alongside the profiling was a kernel campaign: five composed kernels on the batch-one path — a split reduction on the attention rows, a query-head grouping, a mapped truncated draft head, a full-grid target GEMM, and a fused convolution and post-preparation — joined by a sixth, a logit-direct committer. All merged behind fail-closed production contracts. Fifty-two focused composition tests passing. Both offline code generations compiling without register spills.

Measured together on four real SWE-Verified tasks, the composed stack's step wall came out at 1.0016× stock. Null.

The same artifact reports a full-wall throughput ratio of 1.0714, which is a more flattering number and is the wrong one to quote: it moves with acceptance — 4.676 against 4.289 accepted drafts per event between the two arms — not with kernel speed. The step-wall ratio is the kernel-honest measurement and it is 1.0016. The artifact records performance_claim = false and production_default_enabled = false, which is the correct way to bank a result like this.

Six branches, zero merges

The null result forced a disposition review of six outstanding frontier branches. The most useful thing to come out of it was methodological, and it is a check worth stealing: raw ahead-counts systematically overstate outstanding work, because a commit that has been cherry-picked onto the trunk still counts as ahead. Asking git for patch-equivalence instead reduced 18 nominally-outstanding commits to 8 genuinely new ones — and revealed that one branch was fully absorbed already and that another's kernel had landed on the trunk weeks earlier through a different path. Two of the six had zero commits ahead; they were strict ancestors of the trunk.

All six were archived, none merged. Five of the eight genuinely-new commits were on the one branch a standing policy bars, and the rest were priced against ladder rows measured dead. Archiving here means recorded, not deleted — every branch stays on the remote at its named tip and git preserves the history.

Deleting the code that was measured worthless

Three draft and verifier head families were removed from the serving path: a packed pair head, a Tensor Core draft head, and the full-vocabulary verifier head archived on the 2.8 ms figure above. All three were launcher-default off and never production selectors, so removal is behaviour-preserving on every arm. Removing them took the two .cu sources, their builders, codegen checkers, selectors, runners and five test files, plus the environment reads, validators, the mutual-exclusion mode sum, eight state-initialisation counters, a 255-line emitted ready block, a 65-line logits function, two dispatch arms, two fail-closed unwinder arms and two graph-attestation blocks.

The more interesting deletion is the one that removed nothing but wiring — and it is a genuine hazard class worth naming.

The trap flag A deleted lever an operator could still “enable”
The measurement

Two levers were measured below the no-lever baseline in isolation gates — 28.05 and 29.62 tok/s against 32.14 — and their implementations were deleted on 2026-07-25.

The residue

The launcher kept forwarding both environment variables into the container. The declared single-source-of-truth flag registry still described both as queued, three weeks after they were gone.

The failure mode

One would have raised a misleading error. The other was worse: nothing read it any more, so setting it would have been accepted and silently ignored — and an operator would have believed they were measuring a lever that no longer existed.

The fix

Both forwarding lines removed; both registry entries rewritten from queued to deleted, carrying the measured verdict and the deleting commit. A deletion is not finished until the wiring around it is gone too.

That episode is the second time this series has lost real time to a flag that was not what it appeared to be — Vol. VIII documented two correctness fixes that had been silently off for weeks. The lesson has now been paid for twice: the registry is the artifact that has to be correct, because it is the one a future run consults.

Batch Four: A Candidate That Was Faster Without Making Anything Faster

Why this is in a performance paper: it is the measurement failure most likely to be replicated by anyone benchmarking a server, and it nearly shipped a regression here.

Until this window, a batch-four candidate was judged on one number: committed tokens per second, summed over every co-resident request. That number is an aggregate, and it factors exactly:

aggregate throughput  =  per-request rate  ×  requests resident per step

Only the first factor is a property of the kernel stack. The second is a property of how four agentic sessions happened to overlap on that particular run — task-end skew, agent think time, container setup. A candidate can therefore post a large aggregate gain without making anything faster, purely by being measured on a run where more requests were resident.

This is not hypothetical. A two-M candidate cleared the aggregate metric decisively and would have been promoted on it:

The two-M candidate against stock. Same subset, same campaign, two metrics.
MetricWhat it measuresDelta
Aggregate throughputCommitted tokens/sec summed over all co-resident requests+17.2%

The implied co-residency ratio is 1.172 / 0.9704 = 1.2077. The entire aggregate gain is that ratio: the candidate arm was measured with about 21% more requests resident per step, and every individual agent session was slower under it than under stock. Promoting it would have shipped a regression and then baked the scheduling luck into the baseline, so the next candidate would have had to beat a number that was never a real speed.

Promotion now requires both conditions:

Condition 1 — new

Non-regression on the batch-invariant rate

The candidate's per-request step rate must be ≥ stock's. This is the condition that cannot be bought with co-residency, and it is what rejects the two-M shape.

Condition 2 — retained

A real aggregate gain

The candidate's aggregate throughput must be > stock's. A change that moves nothing is not worth its risk.

The asymmetry is deliberate: condition 1 is at least, because a pure co-residency improvement is still real work and must not be punished; condition 2 is strictly greater, because something has to actually move. The phase breakdown now reports both rates per arm and fails loudly if the per-request rate disagrees with aggregate divided by events per step — the identity that makes the decomposition meaningful in the first place.

Two limits are recorded with it so the rule is not overread. It decides promotion, not acceptance — the floor cap is untouched. And the per-request rate is batch-invariant but not co-residency-corrected: it does not say what the candidate would have done at the stock arm's co-residency, because serving more requests concurrently genuinely costs per-request latency. It is a non-regression guard, not a counterfactual. Removing the confound properly needs arms matched on co-residency, which is what a continuous task-pool refill exists to make possible.

A pool that could not hold the batch it was booted for

The refill work turned up a second measurement problem, this one in the configuration. The batch-four arms were booting a key-value pool sized at 40 GiB, which the engine reported as 153,600 tokens — against a four-task working set of roughly 160,000. The pool was undersized by construction, so the tail of the oldest prefix was evicted to admit the newest.

A sixteen-task diagnostic measured the consequence rather than inferring it: once the pool ran full-width, prefix-cache hit rate collapsed to 33–40% while pool utilisation pinned at 75–83%. That combination is diagnostic — a cache thrashing against its own capacity, not a cold cache warming up. Raising the reservation to 46 GiB restores roughly 176,000 tokens and covers the working set with margin. It is a sizing change only: no topology, no sampler, no kernel, no context length, so it cannot move output bytes.

Worth noting for the record is why the fix is sizing and not the flag that nominally addressed exactly this concern. That flag was one of the trap flags of the previous section — measured below baseline, implementation excised weeks earlier, still forwarded, still documented as queued, and read by nothing.

Three portable lessons from batch four None of them is about a kernel
Decompose the metric

If your headline number is a product of a code property and a scheduling property, it will eventually reward the scheduling property. Report both factors and assert the identity between them.

Size for the batch you boot

A cache smaller than the working set does not degrade gracefully; it thrashes, and the signature — low hit rate with high utilisation — is distinguishable from a cold start if you look.

Deployment is a different regime

At batch four the GPU is 97% busy and most steps mix prefill with decode. Levers designed and priced at batch one must be repriced against the decode share of the wall, not assumed to transfer.

Tier B: Admitting Kernels That Cannot Be Byte-Identical

Status: a policy, greenlit under one condition — correct rejection sampling — which is codified below as a hard invariant and made mechanical. No kernel has been qualified under it yet, and nothing on the trunk becomes Tier B by its existence.

Every kernel in this campaign is admitted on byte identity: the candidate's output tensors must be bit-for-bit equal to the incumbent's on a real authenticated event. Call that Tier A. It is the default, it is always preferred, and nothing here weakens it.

Tier A has a structural blind spot, and this volume has been circling it since the attention section. Some kernel changes are byte-impossible by construction rather than by defect — they reassociate a floating-point reduction, and floating-point addition is not associative. The campaign has already measured and rejected on exactly this: a candidate that split the attention reduction into two independent partials and combined them could not match the incumbent's single ordered reduction, and the rejection artifact says so precisely — it cannot satisfy a raw-byte contract in general, even when both paths implement the same real-valued attention formula.

That artifact is the correct Tier A verdict and it stands. But that final clause is the whole motivation for a second tier. Tier A cannot distinguish a kernel that computes something else from a kernel that computes the same thing in a different order. It rejects both, and the ladder puts up to 17.0 ms/step behind that single wall.

What the claim actually is

Speculative decoding is lossless because of the rejection sampler, not because of the drafter. Given a target distribution p and any draft proposal q, the sampler emits samples distributed exactly as p, whatever q is. This is why drafter changes are cheap here: the sampler absorbs them by construction. Tier B does not touch q. It perturbs p itself, into pε. So:

Tier A:   served distribution  ==  p   (exact, by byte identity)

Tier B:   served distribution  ==  pε   (exact, by the sampler)

and   || pε − p ||  is bounded and measured

The served distribution remains exactly lossless with respect to the perturbed verifier, and three gates exist to prove the perturbation is behaviourally immaterial. The policy is explicit that the claim must be stated in those two halves and never collapsed into the single word “lossless.” That distinction is the entire epistemic content of the tier.

The hard invariant: the sampler does not move

If the sampler itself drifts, the first half of that claim evaporates. The served distribution is then neither p nor pε but an unbounded third thing, and two uncontrolled errors compound with no instrument able to separate them. So the sampler's immutability is not a gate to be traded off — it is an invariant, asserted mechanically on every qualification.

The mechanism is worth describing because it is a small, transferable piece of engineering. The three functions in the patcher that write the rejection sampler are located by parsing the syntax tree, not by line number, and their concatenated source is hashed under a per-function banner. The consequences are all intended: an edit anywhere else in the 41,690-line patcher does not move the pin; an edit inside any of the three does; renaming, deleting or reordering one does; and if the patcher ever grows a new sampler-writing function, the extractor fails closed rather than silently leaving the new code unpinned. A run that cannot produce a matching pin is not a failed Tier B candidate — it is not a Tier B run at all, and must not be recorded as one.

The three gates, all required

There is no partial Tier B and no “two of three plus judgement.” A candidate clearing (a) and (b) but not (c) is rejected.

The band's floor is the gate; its ceiling is not a failure condition. A candidate resolving 12 of 16 passes. The upper edge is recorded so a suspiciously high number reads as “replicate before claiming” — the same caution the campaign applies to its own 11/16 high-water mark.

The limits, stated in the policy itself

The most creditable thing about the document is that it bounds its own strength. Three limits are written into it:

Resolution

The band detects a broken agent, not a costly one

At sixteen tasks the 95% Wilson interval on 8/16 spans [0.280, 0.720] — roughly 4.5 to 11.5 resolves. The entire 8–11 band sits inside the interval of its own floor. Gate (c) can catch a candidate that has broken the agent; it cannot detect one that has cost one or two resolves. That is why (a) and (b) are required rather than supporting: the logit bound is the only gate with tight resolution. A Tier B pass is reported as “no behavioural difference detectable at n=16,” never as “no behavioural difference.”

Composition

Two qualified kernels are not a qualified stack

Two independently qualified Tier B kernels in one serving stack have not been qualified together, and their ε bounds do not add in any way the policy models. A stack serving more than one requires its own qualification on the composed stack.

Generality

The bound is measured, not proven

Gate (a) bounds deviation on the captured task. It is not a worst-case bound over inputs and no claim of one should be made from it. A candidate whose deviation is input-dependent in a way the task subsets do not exercise will pass and still be wrong; the only defence offered is the breadth of gate (c) and the stickiness below.

Stickiness

The price of a weaker claim

A qualification binds to an exact triple — the candidate binary, the sampler-region hash, and the serving source commit — and any change to any of the three voids it entirely. No incremental refresh, no inheritance from a sibling in the same family. A Tier A candidate re-proves itself with one byte comparison; a Tier B candidate cannot, because its admission rests on behavioural evidence that does not transfer across binaries.

What Tier B is not about

Two boundaries are drawn sharply. Drafter changes are not Tier B's concern, and the distinction is not one of degree: a drafter change perturbs q, which the sampler corrects exactly and by construction, so it needs no ε bound at all. A verifier change perturbs p, which nothing corrects. And the tier is scoped to verifier-forward kernels only — everything upstream of the sampler. The unifying test is one sentence: a Tier B candidate may perturb the verifier's logits; it may not perturb the mechanism that consumes them.

Nothing here is retroactive. No existing arm becomes Tier B by this document; every arm on the trunk today is Tier A or covered by the drafter exemption, and stays that way. The first candidate is the attention-geometry family — the query-head pairing from the attention section if its byte gate drifts, and if it turns out byte-identical then it is a Tier A candidate and Tier B does not apply to it at all.

And the standing caveat, which the policy states about itself: the full legal ladder is 19.4 ms/step against a 237 ms envelope and cannot close the gap to the cap. Tier B does not change that arithmetic. It unblocks a class of levers. It does not make the campaign's headline target reachable.

Threats to Validity

Honest scope: the robust claims are the relative ones inside the capture — which phase carries the time, which gaps exist, which shapes are faster per byte. The absolute bandwidths are modelled, and the ladder totals inherit that.

No hardware counters

Every bandwidth here is derived

The capture is cuda,cuda-sw,nvtx with no metric sampling, so all bandwidth figures come from measured durations plus a byte model. That model is cross-checked five independent ways and reproduces both the banked GEMM floor to 1.8% and the banked fused-scan model exactly — but it is a model. The one ladder item that genuinely needs counters, the attention L2 persistence window, is marked speculative rather than scored for that reason.

Derived geometry

No model config is present in the tree

Every dimension in the GEMM analysis — hidden size, MLP intermediate, layer split, head count — is inferred from launch grids, kernel template parameters, vocabulary size in the run evidence, and measured durations. The four cross-checks agree, but a published configuration contradicting the hidden dimension would unpick the byte accounting and with it the roofline percentages.

Attribution only

Profiled numbers are not acceptance numbers

The capture carries acceptance_valid=false. The name-lookup call is CUPTI-injected once per eager launch and total profiler overhead is 38.8 ms/step — though it lives on a separate flush thread, and the post-drafter tail contains only 92.8 launches, so its share there is about 0.2 ms and the 10.08 ms idle figure is substantially genuine. Absolute host-side totals elsewhere are inflated and should not be quoted as acceptance evidence.

Shared memory system

The CPU is reading the same DRAM

This is a unified-memory machine: the 273 GB/s is shared between CPU and GPU. Host traffic during the verify forward is a plausible contributor to the 4.26 ms of GEMM jitter, and this trace cannot separate the two. It also means the 85.8%-of-roofline figure may be closer to a practical ceiling than it looks.

The current arm

Landed is not measured

The 232.780 ms/step point is the qrow16 arm of 2026-07-31, not the merged six-kernel composition. That composition has no current-stack byte gate, timing, or acceptance evidence at either batch size, and its own campaign status document says so. Nothing in this volume should be read as a performance claim for the merged stack beyond the composed null.

Modelled wins

The two largest legal items are designs

The host tail (10.08 ms) and the scan fusion (3.45 ms) are modelled from measured quantities, not built and measured. Future Work

The frame: with the step-time ladder priced and short, the interesting work moves to the other two axes — what a step commits, and what the decoder costs in resources that step time does not see.

The ladder is the near-term queue and it is ordered by measured value, not by appeal. But the more important consequence of this volume is that step time is no longer where the leverage is, and two other axes are.

The two axes that step time does not see

Acceptance. Vol. VIII established that the deficit is fixed rather than marginal, which inverts the value of acceptance: a fixed cost is diluted by a better speculator where a marginal one is aggravated by it. This volume hardens that conclusion, because it shows the fixed cost is largely irreducible. If 213 ms/step is close to the floor of what this stack can cost under exact math, then every additional token committed per event is throughput that is kept outright. We sit at 4.754 accepted drafts per event. That is the axis with room on it.

Cache capacity. The finding Vol. VIII closed on remains open and is the most under-instrumented thing in the system. The recurrent state pool is a fixed number of pages, and a speculative decoder must reserve enough to hold its in-flight state. At four concurrent requests the tree reserves 88 pages against native's 24 — leaving 39 cacheable checkpoints against native's 103. The tree does not only cost time per step; it costs prefix-cache capacity, which on an agentic workload full of repeated context is its own throughput term, and one that none of the step-time analysis in this volume captures. The pool-sizing work of the batch-four section is the first measurement to touch it, and it found a cache thrashing at 33–40% hit rate. That is a large number to have been invisible to every instrument in this paper.

Which is the note to end on. This volume is an unusually complete accounting of one axis, and its main finding on that axis is that the axis is nearly exhausted. The costs a system is not instrumenting are the ones most likely to be paying for its wins — and after nine volumes of step-time work, the honest read is that the next result probably does not come from a kernel.
