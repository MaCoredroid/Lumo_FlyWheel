Volume VIII / illustrated systems paper

Marginal Parity

A Speculative Tree Kills Its Per-Event Tax — What's Left Is Fixed, Plus Two Silent Bugs and a Profiling Reversal

Vol. VII left the branched token-tree accepting more tokens than native linear decode and losing on throughput anyway, with the deficit charged to the committer. Nine hundred and eighty-four commits later the picture has changed shape entirely. The tree's per-event cost — the tax it paid for every token it committed, once measured at roughly 140 ms/event against native's 49 — is now statistically indistinguishable from native's. The entire remaining deficit is a fixed per-step term of about 85–90 ms. That is not a smaller gap so much as a different gap: it no longer scales with how much the tree accepts, which makes the two ways to win arithmetic rather than mysterious. Along the way we found the target logits had been at the wrong temperature for seven weeks, a captured CUDA graph had been silently dropping its sampling constraints, and a profiling run at realistic context length dissolved most of an optimization campaign we had been running for days.

TL;DR: the deficit moved from marginal to fixed. A regression over seven tree arms and two native arms puts the tree's per-event slope at +4.6 ms/event over native with a confidence interval of [−15, +24] — the historic ~140 ms/event row tax is confirmed dead (its own interval excludes 140), killed by the kernel work of the last three volumes plus a subtree-parallel scan. What remains is ~85–90 ms/step that does not scale with acceptance, leaving the tree at ~0.92× native at the operating point and two quantified win conditions: cut ~85 ms of fixed cost, or push acceptance to ≥ 5.0. Throughput on our own board went 32.3 → 38.4 → 43.6 tok/s, and one arm touched native's predicted rate — a result we then audited and downgraded ourselves, because the native comparand is a two-point line with zero degrees of freedom. Two silent bugs surfaced: the spec target logits had run at an effective temperature of 0.36 instead of 0.6 since early June, and a warmup graph capture built with an empty sampling config had baked out the top-k/top-p kernels, so live replays sampled an unfiltered tail. And an nsys capture at deployment showed the GPU already 97% busy — retiring an entire class of overlap levers we were about to build.

Abstract

A speculative decoder's cost has two parts: a fixed per-step term paid whether it accepts one token or ten, and a marginal per-event term paid for each speculative event it commits. Which of the two carries your deficit determines what you should optimize, and until this volume we had the answer wrong in a specific and expensive way. Vol. VII charged the tree's throughput loss to the committer — a per-event replay through forty-eight recurrent layers — and an earlier measurement had the tree paying roughly 140 ms/event against native's 49. This volume reports that the marginal tax is gone: fitting step time against acceptance across seven tree arms puts the tree's per-event slope within a few milliseconds of native's, with a confidence interval that excludes the historic figure outright. The kernel work of the last three volumes, plus a subtree-parallel scan that exploits the independence of a tree's disjoint branches, removed it.

What remains is a purely fixed deficit of roughly 85–90 ms per step: the drafter that must propose a twenty-one-node tree where native proposes a five-token chain, the committer's staged span, and host publishes. Because it does not scale with acceptance, the win conditions become arithmetic rather than architectural guesswork — cut about 85 ms of fixed cost and the tree reaches native at equal acceptance, or raise acceptance to 5.0 and it wins at the current cost. Measured throughput on one consistent basis climbed from 32.3 to 43.6 tokens per second across the campaign, and a single arm at the operating point matched what our native fit predicts. We report that arm and then downgrade it ourselves: the native comparand is a two-point line with zero degrees of freedom, confounded across batch size, task set, boot date, and prefill fraction, so “parity” is one consistent reading of the data and not a measured fact. The robust statement is the one we lead with — marginal parity, fixed deficit, ~0.92× native at the operating point.

Two silent bugs surfaced and both are worth the telling. The patcher had been injecting a second sampling-constraint application on top of the stock one, so every speculative arm since early June had been drafting and verifying at an effective temperature of 0.36 rather than the intended 0.6 — seven weeks of absolute numbers quietly off-regime, though the comparisons between arms survive. And a CUDA-graph warmup capture had been constructed with a dummy sampling configuration whose top-k and top-p were unset, so the captured graph omitted the constraint kernels entirely and live replays sampled from an unfiltered tail: rare-token salad, acceptance collapsing to ~1.9, four tasks garbled out of four. Finally, a profiling reversal worth internalizing: the ~26 ms/event of “non-kernel idle” we had spent days chasing turned out to be substantially an artifact of profiling at short context, and a capture at the real deployment point showed the GPU already 97% busy — which retires the entire overlap-lever class we were queuing up to build.

The governing distinction (fixed vs marginal): model step time as step = F + m × eps, where eps is speculative events per step and m is the marginal cost of an event. A deficit in m scales with how well your speculator works — the better it accepts, the more it pays, and no amount of acceptance saves you. A deficit in F is a constant toll that acceptance dilutes: raise accept per event and the fixed cost is amortized over more committed tokens. Discovering that our deficit had moved from m to F is the whole result, because it converts “the tree is structurally slower” into two arithmetic targets — and it makes the accept-beyond-5 work a direct win condition rather than a nice-to-have.

Contribution

One structural result and a great deal of measurement discipline. The result is that a branched speculative verifier's per-event cost on a recurrent hybrid can be brought to parity with a linear chain's — the row tax is not inherent to branching — which relocates the entire deficit into a fixed term and makes the remaining problem arithmetic. The discipline is the rest: a per-step regression that retired our own pooled fit, an instrument that had to be debugged before it could measure, a profiling reversal that killed a lever class, and a self-audit that downgraded our most quotable number.

This page is the sequel to Vol. VII and is self-contained. Like the prior volumes it is a research report rather than an announcement: it keeps the lever ledger with what each bought, a full failure museum of refuted hypotheses and observer effects, and a limits section that states plainly which of our own headlines will not survive contact with a proper native control.

The Shape of the Deficit

The model: step = F + m × eps. Fit step time against speculative events per step, and the intercept F is what you pay for existing while the slope m is what you pay per event. Where your deficit sits decides what you optimize.

Why it matters: a marginal deficit gets worse as your speculator improves. A fixed deficit gets diluted. Same milliseconds, opposite strategy.

For six volumes we treated the tree's slowness as a property of branching — the committer replay, the wider verify, the per-node work — and every one of those is a per-event cost. That framing predicted something uncomfortable: the better the tree got at accepting, the more of these costs it would pay, so its acceptance advantage could never fully convert. Vol. VII's numbers were consistent with exactly that, and an earlier measurement had the tree paying roughly 140 ms per event against native's 49.

Fitting step time against speculative events per step, across seven tree arms spanning every graph mode we ship, gives a different answer:

Step time as a linear function of speculative events per step. The slopes are the per-event cost; the intercepts are the fixed per-step cost.
ArmFitFixed FMarginal m
native (MTP-5)step = 160.4 + 44.6 × eps160.4 ms44.6 ms/event
tree (7 arms, all modes)step = 235.5 + 49.2 × eps235.5 ms49.2 ms/event
difference—+75 ms (fixed)+4.6 ms/event

The slopes are the result. The tree's per-event cost is +4.6 ms over native — against a confidence interval of [−15, +24], which spans zero. Statistically the two lines are parallel: one more accepted event costs the tree what it costs native. The historic ~140 ms/event row tax is not merely reduced, it is excluded by the interval. Whatever the tree still pays, it does not pay it per token.

The entire remaining deficit sits in the intercept, and it is large: about 75 ms/step on this fit, or — on the more robust reading our own audit prefers — a total deficit of 85–90 ms/step at the operating range, measured where the native data is direct rather than extrapolated. That leaves the tree at roughly 0.92× native where it actually runs.

All that separates them is a vertical offset, the fixed per-step cost. A gap that is a slope gets worse as your speculator improves; a gap that is an offset gets amortized away by exactly the thing the tree is good at.

Why the reframe changes the strategy

Throughput is committed tokens over step time, so with the deficit purely fixed the two ways to win fall out as arithmetic. Cut the fixed term — roughly 85 ms — and the tree reaches native at equal acceptance; the fixed term is not mysterious either, being mostly the drafter proposing a twenty-one-node tree where native proposes a five-token chain (~54 ms), the committer's staged span (~39 ms), and host publishes. Or raise acceptance: at the current fixed cost, an accept of 5.0 clears native at the operating point, because every additional token committed per event spreads the same toll over more output. We sit at 4.3–4.75.

That second condition is the one worth dwelling on, because it is the inversion. Under the old marginal-tax picture, the accept-beyond-5 work was a nice-to-have that the row tax would partly eat. Under a fixed-cost picture, acceptance is a direct win condition — the tree's one native advantage now converts cleanly, and every token of accept above the current rate is throughput we keep. The deep suffix tail of Vol. VII stopped being a curiosity and became the shortest path to beating native.

What We Built

The bar for baking a lever: an offline byte gate (the committed state must be bit-identical), a live band gate (the run must land inside the campaign's pass/fail envelope, garble-free), and a speed signal. Nothing ships on a speed number alone.

Why so many small ones: with the deficit now fixed rather than marginal, there is no single 85 ms lever to find. It is a collection of tens of milliseconds, each gated.

The campaign's engineering output is a set of levers now baked as launcher defaults. Each was gated the same way — byte-identical committed state offline, an in-band garble-free live run, and a speed signal — and several are direct descendants of mechanisms the earlier volumes built. The lever that came back from the dead

The truncated drafter head deserves a paragraph, because Vol. VII reported it refuted. The idea — draft from a reduced vocabulary so the drafter reads a fraction of the output-projection weights, while the verifier keeps the full head so nothing is lost — was ranked the top drafter lever and then killed by measurement: the drafter turned out to be host-orchestration-bound, so cutting its largest weight read bought about two percent of a wall dominated by launch glue.

That verdict was correct at the time and stopped being correct once the drafter's host cost was removed. With the spine draft captured as a graph, the glue is gone and what remains is actual weight traffic — at which point halving the read does exactly what the original arithmetic said it would: the drafter falls from 94.9 to 56.3 ms per step. And it is free in quality: on a matched workload the truncated head accepts 5.552 against a full-head control's 5.556, because the 64k gather covers 97.1% of the draft ids actually used at half the bytes.

The general lesson is worth stating plainly, since it is the kind of thing that quietly wastes years: a lever refuted against one bottleneck is not refuted permanently. Removing the dominant cost re-ranks everything behind it, and a measured “no” carries an implicit “while this other thing dominates.” We had recorded the refutation with its reasoning attached, which is the only reason it was cheap to revisit.

Subtree-parallel, and a fixed cost it brought with it

The one lever with genuinely new structure is the subtree-parallel scan. The recurrent scan had been walking all of the tree's nodes serially inside every program, because a scan is inherently sequential along a path. But a tree is only sequential along each path: after the shared prefix, its disjoint branches have no data dependence on one another at all. Partitioning the nodes at runtime from the parent array into a prefix plus K independent subtrees, and giving the subtrees their own program axis, shortens the critical path from the node count to roughly the prefix plus the deepest subtree — and the win grows with wider trees, which is exactly the direction the accept-beyond-5 work pushes.

It also came with an honest complication that the campaign measured rather than assumed. The captured side-stream topology this required carries a fixed +32.7 ms cost per step, while the decomposition itself is worth a real −22.3 ms per event. Those cross over at an acceptance of about 1.47 events per step — well below where we operate — so the lever is a clear net win at the operating point and stays baked. A follow-up that hoped to remove the fixed part by putting the work back on a single stream was refuted by reading the source: the launch was already single-stream, and the 32.7 ms is the per-level launch structure itself.

What we deleted

The campaign also removed machinery. The committer's node-bank burn — the 43 ms of scratch-zeroing that Vol. VII proved redundant — is now deleted outright rather than defaulted off, with its legacy path retired fail-loud. A head-merge seam that had been superseded by the tail design, an internal mechanism that duplicated the subtree preseed, and a decode kind that no longer had a purpose all went with it. This matters for a reason beyond tidiness: every one of those was a live branch that a future measurement could have silently taken, and this series has now lost enough weeks to configuration that could reach an unintended path that deleting is cheaper than guarding.

Two Silent Bugs

What makes these worth telling: neither crashed, neither tripped a gate, and both lived inside code that had been reviewed and shipped. One changed every number we published for seven weeks; the other made a captured graph quietly stop doing part of its job.

Two defects surfaced in this campaign that had been running silently for a long time. They are unrelated in mechanism but share a shape: a piece of configuration that was absent rather than wrong, in a place where absence had a plausible-looking effect instead of an error.

Bug one: seven weeks at the wrong temperature

Our patcher injects a sampling-constraint application into the speculative verify path. It had been written against an image whose stock rejection sampler did not apply constraints itself, and its guard checked only for the patcher's own copy — not for a stock one. When the stock file later grew its own application, the guard did not notice, and both fired. Applying a temperature scaling twice does not apply it twice as hard in a linear way; it squares it. A requested temperature of 0.6 became an effective 0.6² = 0.36, and had been so for every speculative arm since the fifth of June — roughly seven weeks of campaigns.

A temperature that is too low is a particularly quiet bug, because nothing breaks. The model simply samples more greedily than intended; drafts get easier to guess, acceptance drifts up, and every arm is wrong in the same direction, so A/B comparisons keep looking sane. There was even a subtler side effect: the second application landed asymmetrically, so parent rows were being evaluated at 0.36 while the tree's own rows saw 0.6 — an inconsistency inside a single arm that no gate was watching for.

The fix applies the constraint only when the stock path lacks it, with a patch-time escape hatch (FR13_TEMP_LEGACY_DOUBLE=1) that reproduces the old double-apply byte for byte — deliberately kept, because the honest way to re-base seven weeks of numbers is an A/B against the bug rather than a declaration. That A/B is reassuring: at true 0.6 the best arm accepts 4.331 against the 0.36 control's 4.434, a difference of about a tenth of a token. The comparisons the campaign made survive; the absolute regime they were made in does not, and every pre-fix number now carries the caveat.

Figure 2Two absences with plausible symptoms: a guard that checked the wrong thing, and a capture built from an empty config

Figure 2 shows both bugs as the same species. In (A) a guard tested for the wrong thing, so a constraint ran twice and squared a temperature — a change with no error and a plausible-looking effect. In (B) a graph was captured from a configuration whose constraints were unset, so the recorded graph simply had no constraint kernels in it; every replay afterwards sampled an unfiltered distribution at full speed. Neither failed loudly. Both were found only by measurements that could not be explained any other way.

Bug two: a captured graph that forgot to filter

The second bug is a CUDA-graph hazard worth generalizing. To capture the sampler into a graph we build a warmup step with a dummy sampling configuration. That dummy had top_k and top_p unset. A graph records the kernels that actually execute during capture — so with no constraints present, the captured graph contained no top-k or top-p kernels at all. Every subsequent live replay ran at full speed and sampled from the raw, unfiltered tail of the distribution.

The symptom was spectacular and, for a while, misleading: rare-token salad in the output, acceptance collapsing to about 1.9, and four of four tasks garbling. It looked like a state-corruption bug — exactly the family this series has chased since Vol. V — and the hunt went there first, through a long arc of paired-replay probes comparing a live replay against a staged rerun on the same inputs. Those probes produced a per-row misalignment signature, then an apparent exoneration that we later had to retract because only one arm's random state had been pinned.

What cracked it was pinning both sides. With the randomness identical on both arms, the walk products came out byte-identical while the emitted tokens still diverged. That combination is decisive: if the state and the walk and the random draws all agree and the tokens do not, the difference cannot be in the state, the walk, or the RNG — it has to be in the sampling distribution itself. From there the missing constraint kernels were a short step. The fix captures with real constraint tensors so the kernels are recorded, and refills their values from live metadata each step, because a graph bakes addresses rather than numbers. Garble went to zero, output returned to coherent English, and on the first clean boot the agent landed a genuine one-character fix in a real file with its tests passing.

The general lesson

Capture-time configuration is part of a graph's semantics. If a knob is unset when you capture, its kernel does not exist at replay — silently, and at full speed.

Measuring the Measurement

The theme: for most of this campaign the obstacle was not the GPU. It was knowing what our own numbers meant — and three separate times, a result we had been optimizing against turned out to be a property of how we were measuring.

Three measurement stories, in the order they happened, because each one changed what we worked on next.

The slope ladder that wasn't

We ship the speculative step in several CUDA-graph modes, and arm-level numbers appeared to rank them cleanly — a “ladder” of throughput-per-event slopes that made one fusion mode look clearly best and justified building more fusion. Pooling seven arms into a single regression dissolved it: all four modes lie on one line, step = 235.5 + 49.2 × eps, within a couple of milliseconds. The ladder was an eps artifact — the arms had been running at different acceptance rates, and comparing their aggregate throughput was comparing points along one curve rather than different curves. The graph-fusion work it had been justifying was moving nothing, and we stopped it.

Then the pooled fit itself failed. Nine arm-level points cannot separate an intercept from a slope with any confidence — the honest mode-detection floor at arm level turned out to be about ±100 ms, not the ±15 we had been quoting. Replacing it with a per-step regression — thousands of individual steps, each with its own known draft count, from an instrumented sidecar — gave real intervals and showed the pooled fit had been wrong in both directions at once: step = 217.1 + 59.0 × drafts at R² = 0.94. The pooled fit was retired.

That instrument had to be debugged before it could measure, which is its own small lesson. The first version wrote per-step arrays into a file that the harness rewrote every second, so a run with five hundred timed steps reported zero samples; the second lost its data to the container teardown's kill signal. Only the third — a separate sidecar file, throttled dumps, and a poller catching the rewrite boundary — actually retained what it recorded.

The idle that dissolved

The per-step regression exposed something real and alarming: roughly 28–30 ms per event of non-kernel time inside the verify span. Time inside our own timing brackets that no kernel accounted for. We made that the campaign, and worked it as an elimination:

The last one is where it turned. Profiling on live tasks at realistic context length showed the verify span is mostly real kernels after all: attention at 77 ms per draft against 0.9 ms at the short contexts we had been profiling, plus about 158 ms of GEMM, 29.5 of output projection, and 9.9 of scan. The ~26 ms/event of “idle” was substantially an artifact of profiling at short context, where the attention kernels are nearly free and the fixed overheads dominate the picture. The phenomenon we had been attacking for days was, in large part, a property of the bench.

This is not a wasted campaign — the eliminations are real, the instruments now exist, and a genuine fixed side-stream cost was found and quantified along the way. But the floor moved up: much of what looked like recoverable overhead is attention work that scales with context, and the honest lever list was rebuilt around kernel efficiency rather than gap-hunting.

The deployment capture that retired a lever class

The last measurement is the one with the widest implications. Everything above was profiled at batch one, where a single request's structure is legible. Capturing at the actual deployment point — four concurrent requests, the configuration we ship — showed the GPU is already 97% busy. There is no idle to overlap into. An entire class of levers we had queued, the overlap and async-hiding family, is dead at deployment regardless of how well it profiles at batch one.

The capture also showed why single-request intuitions mislead here: most steps at deployment are mixed, with prefill and decode sharing the same weight passes, so a pure-decode model covers only the decode share of the wall clock. What survives as regime-robust are the levers whose benefit does not depend on finding gaps: acceptance, and the per-row efficiency of the decode kernels themselves. The working rule we adopted is to design at batch one, where mechanisms are visible, but to price every lever by its decode share at deployment — because a millisecond saved in a gap that does not exist at B=4 is a millisecond of nothing.

What survived

Per-step regression with real intervals, a working sidecar instrument, a quantified fixed side-stream cost, and a lever list ranked by profile data rather than intuition.

The parity claim, and why we downgraded it

At that arm's operating point our native fit predicts about 43.7 tokens per second. The tree measured 43.57. Read literally, the tree reached native throughput for the first time in this series — and the temptation to lead with that sentence is exactly why the campaign ran a seven-agent audit against its own claims. The audit's verdict on this one is unambiguous, and we are reporting it rather than burying it:

So “the tree reached native parity” is one consistent reading of the data and not a measured fact. What survives the audit is the pair of statements this volume actually leads with, both of which hold under the wide intervals: the marginal per-event difference is statistically indistinguishable from zero and excludes the historic 140 ms/event, and the total deficit at the operating range is 85–90 ms/step — measured where the native data is direct rather than extrapolated — leaving the tree at about 0.92× native. The best arm is real and it is the best we have measured; it is not yet a parity result.

Family A — artifacts: real measurements of the wrong thing.

The graph-mode slope ladder

contaminated

Why it looked right: arm-level throughput ranked our CUDA-graph modes in a clean order, with a consistent spread. It read as a real ranking and justified further fusion work.

  • What killed it: pooling the arms into one regression put all four modes on a single line within a couple of milliseconds. The arms had simply been running at different acceptance rates — the “ladder” was different points on one curve. The fusion work it justified was moving nothing.

Lesson: if throughput depends on a covariate, comparing aggregates across arms that differ in that covariate ranks the covariate, not the arms. Regress; don't rank.

The ~26 ms/event of in-span idle

superseded

Why it looked right: a per-step regression with thousands of samples showed substantial non-kernel time inside our own verify-span brackets. It was reproducible, it was large, and it survived several eliminations — so it became the campaign.

  • What killed it: profiling on live tasks at realistic context showed the verify span is mostly real kernels: attention alone runs 77 ms per draft at real context against 0.9 ms at the short contexts we had been profiling. The “idle” was substantially an artifact of the bench, and the floor moved up.

Lesson: profile at the context length you deploy at. A short prompt makes attention nearly free, which inflates every fixed overhead's apparent share and can manufacture a phantom gap.

The overlap-lever class

settled

Why it looked right: at batch one there were visible gaps, and a queue of designs existed to hide host work behind GPU work. Overlap is the standard answer to a gap.

  • What killed it: a capture at the actual deployment point — four concurrent requests — showed the GPU already 97% busy, with most steps mixing prefill and decode so they share weight passes. There is no idle to overlap into. The whole class is dead where we ship, however well it profiles at batch one.

Lesson: design at batch one where mechanisms are visible, but price every lever by its share at the deployment point. A millisecond saved in a gap that does not exist at concurrency is nothing.

The eleven-pass drafter model

superseded

Why it looked right: counters suggested the drafter was making about eleven sequential passes, which would have made pass-count the dominant drafter lever and implied a specific, attractive fix.

  • What killed it: the counters were misread — the tail is retrieval-sourced and engaged nearly always, so the counter was measuring something else entirely. A related proposal to cut the deep tail for speed turned out to be an anti-lever: the tail carries 0.85–1.31 of the acceptance, so removing it costs more than it saves.

Lesson: before optimizing against a counter, confirm what increments it. And check whether the thing you plan to cut is carrying the benefit you are trying to protect.

Family B — eliminations: good hypotheses the instruments killed.

Host preparation before launch

settled

Why it looked right: the natural explanation for non-kernel time inside a span is host work landing between the span's opening and the actual launch — input preparation, metadata assembly, the usual suspects.

  • What killed it: splitting the span into a host half and an execution half measured the host side at essentially zero. The idle sat entirely after enqueue.

Lesson: split the bracket before theorizing about what is inside it. One extra event answers the question that a week of hypotheses cannot.

A host tail after enqueue

settled

Why it looked right: if not before the launch, then after it — a host tail folded into a late stop event would look exactly like in-span idle.

  • What killed it: a CPU-clock discriminator at the span end measured a tail of 0.4–0.5 ms against 267 ms of execution. Conclusive, and it pushed the search inside the captured graph.

Lesson: a discriminator that separates two hypotheses by three orders of magnitude is worth building even when you expect it to say “neither.”

Silent fallback to piecewise graphs

settled

Why it looked right: a step that quietly fell back from a full captured graph to piecewise execution would carry exactly the kind of overhead that no kernel table shows.

  • What killed it: tagging every step with its dispatch mode returned full-graph on 6329 of 6329 steps. No fallbacks at all.

Lesson: the cheapest way to test “is it silently doing the slow thing” is to make the system state its own mode on every step.

Single-streaming the subtree scan

settled

Why it looked right: the subtree-parallel lever brought a fixed +32.7 ms side-stream cost; putting the same kernels back on one stream looked like a clean way to keep the win and drop the tax.

  • What killed it: a source read, before any build — the launch was already single-stream. The 32.7 ms is the per-level launch structure itself, not stream overhead.

Lesson: read the code path before designing a fix for what you assume it does. The cheapest refutation in this whole volume cost one file read.

Step-graph capture as a speed lever

superseded

Why it looked right: capturing the whole step into one graph projected substantial savings from eliminated launch overhead, and the projection was arithmetically sound.

  • What killed it: both arms landed on the pooled line — the speed projections did not materialize, because the host cost had already been hoisted out to make capture legal in the first place. Only launch gaps remained, and they were below the detection floor. The work delivered a correctness milestone and stays off as a speed lever.

Lesson: when a prerequisite for a lever removes the cost the lever targets, the lever's projection is already spent. Re-derive the projection after the prerequisite lands.

Family C — observer effects: the instrument as adversary.

A debug rider that doubled its own span

contaminated

Why it looked right: an arm carrying a detailed per-row trace produced a committer span of 106 ms against a normal 42 — a dramatic and apparently important regression.

  • What killed it: the trace itself. Its per-row device-to-host copies were serializing the very span it was measuring. The arm's correctness results stand; its speed numbers were struck from the record and re-run on a clean arm.

Lesson: a diagnostic that touches the device inside the region it times is not measuring that region. Keep speed arms and trace arms separate, and mark trace-arm timings unquotable at the source.

The sampler that lost its samples

contaminated

Why it looked right: the per-step instrument was the right idea and eventually produced the volume's central result. Its first two versions simply reported nothing.

  • What killed them: version one wrote into a file the harness rewrote every second, so a run with hundreds of timed steps reported zero samples; version two lost everything to the container teardown's kill signal. Only a separate sidecar with throttled dumps and a boundary-catching poller actually retained data.

Lesson: an instrument's persistence path is part of the instrument. Validate that it survives the harness's own lifecycle before trusting a null result from it.

The exoneration we had to retract

contaminated

Why it looked right: during the garble hunt, a paired-replay probe appeared to clear the captured replay path: its outputs differed from the staged rerun's only in ways that randomness could explain.

  • What killed it: only one arm's random state had been pinned. With both sides pinned the comparison inverted — identical draws, identical walk products, still-divergent tokens — which is what finally localized the bug to the sampling distribution. The exoneration was self-corrected in the record.

Lesson: “the difference is explained by randomness” is only admissible when the randomness is pinned on both arms. A half-pinned comparison can exonerate the guilty.

Reflection: When the Instrument Is the Subject

Worth stating plainly: the single most valuable artifact this campaign produced is not a kernel. It is a per-step regression with real confidence intervals, and the discipline to let it retire our own earlier claims.

Nine hundred commits produced one structural result — the deficit moved from marginal to fixed — and a great deal of evidence about how such a result gets found. Nearly every wrong turn in this volume came from the same place: a number that was real, reproducible, and about something other than what we thought. The graph-mode ladder was real and was about acceptance rates. The in-span idle was real and was substantially about context length. The batch-one gaps are real and are about batch one. None of these were sloppy measurements; each was a correct measurement of the wrong quantity, which is a much harder failure to notice because it survives repetition.

The defense that actually worked was structural rather than careful: fit a model with a covariate in it, and report intervals. The moment step time was regressed against acceptance instead of compared across arms, the ladder dissolved, the pooled fit was exposed as wrong in both directions, and the fixed-versus-marginal split — the thing that reframed the whole project — fell out of the same fit. Intervals then did the second half of the job by telling us which of our own headlines to retire, including the most quotable one in this volume.

Threats to Validity

Honest scope: the robust claim is the marginal one — the row tax is dead, with an interval that excludes it. Nearly everything else in this volume is a point estimate with wide intervals, and we say so where it matters.

The native control

Two points, zero degrees of freedom

Our native line is an exact fit through two arms whose slope is identified by a single batch-4 versus batch-8 contrast, with batch size, task set, boot date, and prefill fraction all varying together — and one of the two points had three of four tasks exit early. Every native comparison in this volume, including the parity reading, inherits that weakness. A properly-powered native sweep is the single highest-value measurement we do not yet have.

Wide intervals

Point estimates doing heavy lifting

The fixed-deficit interval spans [32, 118] ms and the marginal difference spans [−15, +24] ms/event. “The deficit is fixed” is well supported at the level of which term carries it; the precise 85–90 ms figure is a working number, not a measured constant.

Single arms

Best-board results are one run each

The 43.57 arm, like most cells on the board, is a single run at one prefill fraction, on a four-task subset, with workload-phase effects unquantified. The campaign's band gate catches gross breakage, not run-to-run variance of a few percent.

Re-based history

Seven weeks of numbers carry a caveat

Every pre-fix absolute number was taken at an effective temperature of 0.36. The controlled A/B suggests arm-to-arm comparisons survive — accept moved about a tenth of a token — but any absolute figure from that period should be read as off-regime, and the new reference band starts after the fix.

Future Work

The frame: with the deficit fixed, every remaining lever is worth exactly its milliseconds divided by the step — and acceptance is worth more than it used to be, because it now dilutes the toll instead of multiplying it.

The two win conditions are arithmetic: cut roughly 85 ms of fixed per-step cost, or reach an acceptance of 5.0. Profiling at real context rebuilt the lever ranking around actual kernel cost rather than gap-hunting, and it puts the work in a clear order.

A new front: the tree is crowding out the prefix cache

The newest finding is not a speed lever at all, and it connects this work back to Vol. IV. The recurrent state pool is a fixed number of pages, and a speculative decoder must reserve enough of them to hold its in-flight state. A five-token chain reserves few; a twenty-one-node tree reserves many. At four concurrent requests the tree reserves 88 pages against native's 24 — and since the pool is finite, that leaves 39 cacheable checkpoints against native's 103.

In other words the tree does not only cost time per step; it costs prefix-cache capacity, which on an agentic workload full of repeated context is its own throughput term — one that none of the step-time analysis in this volume captures. Two ungated fixes exist for it, capping the node-bank reservation and the speculative block allocation, with a real trade-off against block size to be measured rather than assumed. The measure-first sequence is written; the measurement has not run. It is a reminder that a decoder's cost has more axes than its step time, and that the ones you are not instrumenting are the ones most likely to be paying for your wins.
